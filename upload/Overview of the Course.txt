Let us now look at what citizenship means in the Indian Constitution. Now, citizenship is the
status of a person recognized under law as being a legal member of a sovereign state or
belonging to a nation.
In India, Articles 5 to 11 of the Constitution deals with this concept of citizenship. Now, the
term citizenship entails the enjoyment of full membership of any state in which a citizen has
civil and political rights. First, we shall discuss all the articles in the Indian Constitution
pertaining to citizenship.
Let us look at Article 5, which talks of citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution.
Now, what I mean here is that this article talks about citizenship for people at the
commencement of the Constitution, that is 26 January 1950. Under this, citizenship is
conferred upon those people who have their domicile in Indian Territory and 1. who was born
in Indian Territory or 2. whose either parent was born in Indian Territory or 3. who has
ordinarily been a resident of India for not less than 5 years immediately preceding the
commencement of the Constitution.
Let us look at Article 6, which talks of the citizenship of certain persons who have migrated
from Pakistan. Any person who has migrated from Pakistan shall be a citizen of India at the
time of the commencement of the Constitution if 1. he or either of his parents or any of his
grandparents was born in India as given in the Government of India Act of 1935 and 2. a. in
case such a person has migrated before July 19, 1948 and has been ordinarily a resident of
India since his migration or b. in case such a person has migrated after July 19, 1948 and he
has been registered as a citizen of India by an officer appointed in that behalf of the
Government of the Dominion of India on an application made by him thereof to such an
officer before the commencement of the Constitution but this is provided that no person shall
be so registered unless he has been resident of India for at least 6 months preceding the date
of his application. Now Article 7 looks at the citizenship of certain migrants to Pakistan and
this Article deals with the rights of people who had migrated to Pakistan after March 1, 1947
but then subsequently returned to India.
Article 8 looks at the citizenship of certain persons of Indian origin residing outside India. Now
this Article deals with the rights of the people of Indian origin who are residing outside India
for purposes of employment, marriage and education. Article 9 looks at people who are
voluntarily acquiring citizenship of a foreign country will not be citizens of India and Article 10
states that any person who is considered a citizen of India under any of the provisions of this
part shall continue to be citizens and will also be subject to any law made by the Parliament.
Article 11 states that the Parliament will regulate the right of citizenship by law that the
Parliament therefore has the right to make any provision concerning the acquisition and
termination of citizenship and any other matter relating to citizenship. Now let us look at the
citizenship of India constitutional provisions. Citizenship in India is therefore governed as we
just said in the Articles 5 to 11 in Part 3 of the Indian Constitution.
The Citizenship Act 1955 is the legislation that is dealing with citizenship. Now this Act has

Amendment Act of 1992, the Citizenship Amendment Act of 2003, the Citizenship

Let us look at the Citizenship Act of 1955 which talks about how the citizenship of India can

Number 3. Citizenship by descent. Number 4. Citizenship by registration. Number 5.
Citizenship by naturalization.
Number 6. By incorporation of territory by the Government of India. And so people who were

Let us look at termination of Indian citizenship. Termination of citizenship is possible in three

If any citizen of India who is also a national of another country renounces his Indian citizenship

Termination of Indian citizenship can also happen if a citizen knowingly or voluntarily adopts

The Indian Constitution is the supreme law of India, laying down the framework that defines
the political principles, establishes the structure, procedures, powers, and duties of
government institutions, and sets out fundamental rights, directive principles, and duties of
citizens.
 Adopted: November 26, 1949
 Effective From: January 26, 1950 (celebrated as Republic Day)
 Length: The longest written constitution in the world, originally consisting of 395

 Type: A written, federal, parliamentary, and democratic constitution with a quasifederal structure.

Key Features
 Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic: Defines India’s nature and

 Fundamental Rights: Guarantees basic freedoms like speech, religion, and equality to
all citizens.
 Directive Principles of State Policy: Guidelines to the government to promote social

Background to the Framing of the Indian Constitution
The framing of the Indian Constitution was a historic process rooted in India’s struggle for
independence and the need to establish a democratic governance system for a diverse society.
Lets look at the Historical COntext -
 India gained independence from British rule on August 15, 1947.
 A need arose for a comprehensive framework that would unify a diverse country and
secure democratic governance.
 The Constituent Assembly was formed in 1946 to draft the constitution.
 It took 2 years, 11 months, and 18 days to complete the draft.
Colonial Legacy and Need for Self-Governance
 India was under British colonial rule for nearly two centuries.
 By the early 20th century, Indians increasingly demanded self-rule (Swaraj) and
political rights.
 Various acts like the Government of India Acts (1919, 1935) introduced limited selfgovernance but fell short of full democracy.
 The Indian National Congress and other political groups pushed for complete
independence.
Indian Independence Movement
 The demand for independence intensified with leaders like Mahatma Gandhi
advocating nonviolent resistance.
 World War II further weakened British control.
 After the war, Britain agreed to transfer power to Indians.
Formation of the Constituent Assembly (1946)
 The Constituent Assembly of India was formed to draft a constitution.
 It was elected indirectly by provincial assemblies (members were mostly from
political parties like the Congress and the Muslim League).
 The Assembly consisted of 389 members initially, later reduced after partition.
Key Influences on the Constitution
 The Constitution drew inspiration from various sources:
o British Constitution: Parliamentary system, rule of law, parliamentary
sovereignty.
o US Constitution: Fundamental rights, judicial review, federalism.
o Irish Constitution: Directive Principles of State Policy.
o Canadian Constitution: Federation with a strong central government.
o Weimar Constitution of Germany: Fundamental rights and duties.
 Indian traditions like Panchayati Raj (local self-governance) also influenced the
framework.
Role of the Constituent Assembly
 Chaired by Dr. Rajendra Prasad, with Dr. B.R. Ambedkar as the Chairman of the
Drafting Committee.
 The Assembly met from December 1946 to November 1949.
 Members debated, discussed, and refined the draft constitution in multiple sessions.
 The aim was to create a document that could unite diverse communities and regions
while ensuring democracy and justice.
Partition and Its Impact
 During the drafting process, British India was partitioned into India and Pakistan
(August 1947).
 This led to a reduction in Constituent Assembly members and influenced provisions
related to minorities and federal structure.
Women Members of the Constituent Assembly - The Constituent Assembly, tasked with
drafting India’s Constitution, had only 15 women members out of 389, reflecting the
social norms of the time but still marking an important step for women’s participation in
shaping India’s future.
Here are some of the important women who were there -

2. Hansa Mehta

Contributions of Women Members
 Advocacy for Gender Equality: Women members ensured that the Constitution
explicitly guaranteed equal rights for women, including the right to vote, property
rights, and protection against discrimination.
 Social Justice and Welfare: They emphasized social reforms, education for women,
and the elimination of practices like child marriage and discrimination.
 Influence on Fundamental Rights: Hansa Mehta, in particular, played a pivotal role in
modifying the Universal Declaration of Human Rights’ language to be more inclusive
of women’s equality.
Challenges Faced
 Despite their efforts, women were a minority and had to assert their voices in a maledominated Assembly.
 Many social customs and patriarchal attitudes influenced debates, limiting the scope
of some reforms.
Legacy
 Their participation laid the foundation for women’s legal rights and empowerment in
independent India.
 They inspired future generations of women leaders and lawmakers.
Constituent Assembly Debates (CAD) — a crucial part of India’s constitutional
history- are the recorded discussions, arguments, and deliberations of the members of
the Constituent Assembly of India during the drafting of the Indian Constitution
between 1946 and 1950.
Significance of the Debates
 The CAD provides insight into the intentions, ideas, and visions of the framers of the
Constitution.
 It reveals how contentious issues were resolved, such as federalism, fundamental
rights, minority protections, and the role of religion.
 Courts in India often refer to the CAD to interpret the meaning and scope of
constitutional provisions.
 The debates serve as a historical and legal document illuminating the socio-political
context of the time.
Key Features of the Constituent Assembly Debates
1. Duration and Scope
o The Assembly met for approximately three years from December 9, 1946, to
November 26, 1949.
o Discussions covered every aspect of the Constitution: structure of government,
rights, duties, judiciary, emergency powers, and more.
2. Prominent Participants
o Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (Chairman of the Drafting Committee)
o Jawaharlal Nehru (First Prime Minister)
o Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel
o Rajendra Prasad (President of the Assembly)
o Alladi Krishnaswamy Iyer
o K.M. Munshi, among others.
3. Themes Discussed
o Fundamental Rights: Debated at length, especially on freedom of speech,
religion, and equality.
o Directive Principles of State Policy: Non-justiciable guidelines aimed at social
welfare.
o Federalism: Balancing powers between the Centre and States.
o Minority Rights: Protections for religious, linguistic, and cultural minorities.
o Language: Choice of official language(s).
o Reservation Policies: Affirmative action for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled
Tribes, and other backward classes.
4. Challenges and Conflicts
o The Assembly debated contentious issues like the position of princely states,
integration of Jammu & Kashmir, and partition-related challenges.
o There were also discussions on the role of religion in the state and the right to
property.
5. Documentation
o The debates were meticulously recorded and published in multiple volumes.
o They serve as a key resource for scholars, lawyers, and historians.
Importance Today
 The CAD is frequently cited by the Supreme Court and High Courts in India to
interpret constitutional provisions.
 It helps in understanding the original intent behind the provisions.
 Offers valuable lessons on democratic deliberation and consensus-building.

Introduction to Indian Constitution
Preamble to the Indian Constitution
Hello and welcome to this exciting journey where we will today dive deep into one of the most
inspiring parts of the Indian Constitution — the Preamble. I'm Dr. Sneha Yadav, and together
we will explore not just 85 words, but words that changed the destiny of over a billion people
and continues to shape the world's largest democracy today.
Imagine -watching a movie. Before the story unfolds, you see a trailer that gives you a glimpse
of what to expect — the drama, the heroes, the emotions etc. The Preamble of the Indian
Constitution works the same way. It is the trailer or the opening scene that introduces the main
themes and promises of our Constitution. It's the constitutional DNA of India, carrying within
its brief yet profound text which forms the genetic code of our democracy.
You can also think of the Preamble as the opening chord of a magnificent symphony. Just as
that first note sets the tone for everything that follows, the Preamble establishes the
philosophical foundation upon which our entire Constitution stands. But here's what makes the
Indian Preamble truly extraordinary—it's not merely an introduction; it's a revolutionary
manifesto wrapped in legal language.
To understand its significance, let's travel back to ancient times. Even the great Roman Empire
had its founding documents, but they were declarations of imperial power. The Magna Carta
of 1215 limited the king's power, but it didn't empower the people. The American Constitution's
preamble begins with "We the People," but when it was written in 1787, "people" excluded
enslaved individuals, women, and Native Americans.
The Indian Preamble, however, was drafted by representatives of a nation where every adult—
regardless of caste, creed, gender, or economic status would have the right to vote from day
one. This was revolutionary not just for India, but for the entire world in 1949.
Today, we'll discover how these words came to be, what they mean, and why they still matter
in your life today.
Let’s Read It Together!
"WE, THE PEOPLE OF INDIA, having solemnly resolved to constitute India into a
SOVEREIGN SOCIALIST SECULAR DEMOCRATIC REPUBLIC and to secure to all its
citizens: JUSTICE, social, economic and political; LIBERTY of thought, expression, belief,
faith and worship; EQUALITY of status and of opportunity; and to promote among them all
FRATERNITY assuring the dignity of the individual and the unity and integrity of the Nation;
IN OUR CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY this twenty-sixth day of November, 1949, do
HEREBY ADOPT, ENACT AND GIVE TO OURSELVES THIS CONSTITUTION."
While the Constitution is a detailed legal document listing laws and rights, the Preamble sets
the tone — it tells us what this document stands for and the purpose behind it. Words like
Justice, Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity jump out not just as ideals to admire, but as
commitments to live by.
K.M. Munshi, one of the brilliant minds in our Constituent Assembly, called the Preamble "the
keynote to the Constitution." But let me share with you a lesser-known story about Munshi.
During the Partition riots, when communal violence was tearing the nation apart, Munshi
insisted that the word "secular" must eventually find its way into our Preamble. He believed
that without this principle, India would not survive as a unified nation.
Did you know? The Preamble underwent 2,473 amendments during the drafting process but
only one formal amendment was accepted which gave us the words Socialist" and "Secular.
Every single word was debated, discussed, and deliberated upon. The framers spent 114 days
just perfecting these 85 words.
Why the Preamble Matters Today?
Although the Preamble is part of a historical document, it is not frozen in time. It is a living
promise that continues to inspire and guide India today. Whenever there’s debate over laws or
rights, the courts often refer back to the Preamble to see the original vision for the nation. It
reminds politicians and citizens alike of the ideals they must uphold. For example, movements
for social justice, women’s rights, and freedom of expression all draw strength from the
Preamble’s promise. It is a beacon that lights the way for progress, justice, and peace.
To truly understand why the Preamble is so important, we need to travel back to the historic
year of 1947 – the year India gained independence after centuries of foreign rule. As India
woke up to freedom, the leaders faced a huge challenge — how to unite a country of over 300
million people speaking hundreds of languages, following different religions, and coming from
diverse cultural backgrounds?
The framers of the Constitution knew that laws alone could not keep this giant nation together.
They needed a strong foundation of shared values and vision. So, the Preamble was created as
a solemn declaration — a rallying cry to unite all Indians under common ideals. It was their
mission statement for the new India, announcing to the world and to every citizen the values
that would guide the nation forward.
Let me now take you back to December 13, 1946. Picture the scene: Jawaharlal Nehru stands
before the Constituent Assembly and moves what he calls the "Objective Resolution." This
wasn't just another political speech—this was the moment when the idea of modern India was
formally born.
Nehru's words that day were poetry in motion: At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the
world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom. A moment comes, which comes but rarely
in history, when we step out from the old to the new, when an age ends, and when the soul of
a nation, long suppressed, finds utterance.
"At the dawn of history, India started on her unending quest, and trackless centuries are filled
with her striving and the grandeur of her success and her failures. Through good and ill fortune
alike, she has never lost sight of that quest or forgotten the ideals which gave her strength."
But here's a remarkable detail that many don't know: Nehru wrote this resolution by hand, in
the early hours of the morning, by candlelight. The power had gone out in his residence, but he
was so passionate about capturing India's constitutional vision that he continued writing by the
flickering flame.
Now, imagine this moment: 299 members of the Constituent Assembly, led by Dr. B.R.
Ambedkar, the chief architect of our Constitution, knew they needed something powerful to
begin with. Something that would capture the dreams and aspirations of a newly independent
nation emerging from centuries of colonial rule.
Nehru called it "the soul of the Constitution and the very spirit of it." But here's something that
might surprise you - the Preamble we know today wasn't the original version.
The words "Socialist" and "Secular" were actually added much later, in 1976, through the 42nd
Amendment during Indira Gandhi's Emergency period. The original Preamble was adopted on
November 26, 1949. The forefathers of the Indian Constitution succeeded brilliantly. What
they created wasn't just a legal document - it was a promise. A promise that begins with three
of the most revolutionary words in Indian history: "We, the People.
Now, let's understand Preamble word to word, because every single word here is revolutionary.
Notice the phrase “We, the people”? This shows that India is ruled by its citizens, not by a king
or foreign power. This idea of popular sovereignty was revolutionary and radical at that
time power truly belongs to the people.We, the People of India".
These opening words are perhaps the most revolutionary in the entire Constitution. In 1949,
when monarchies still ruled much of the world, and when colonial empires were just beginning
to crumble, the idea that power resided in the people was truly radical.
But let's dig deeper into the phrase "People of India." During the Constituent Assembly debates,
there was intense discussion about who constituted "the people." Some members argued for
"citizens," others for "inhabitants." The choice of "people" was deliberate—it included
everyone who called India home, regardless of their legal status.
Dr. B.R. Ambedkar explained this choice with characteristic clarity: "The people are not just
those who vote, but all those whose lives are affected by the Constitution. A child who cannot
vote is still part of 'the people.' A person who has not yet acquired citizenship but lives here is
still part of 'the people.'" and thats how we incorporated the powerful words “ we the people of
India”.
Next, we hear about the kind of country India aims to be — sovereign, socialist, secular,
democratic, and a republic. Each of these words is like a building block of our nation’s identity.
Sovereign – The Power Belongs To Whom?
The word sovereign means complete independence. After centuries of colonial rule, where
foreign rulers controlled India’s decisions, sovereignty was a powerful assertion — India would
now decide its destiny independently. When the framers chose this word, they were not just
declaring independence from British rule—they were asserting India's right to determine its
own destiny without interference from any external power.
Here's a powerful story that illustrates what sovereignty meant to our leaders: When Lord
Mountbatten suggested that India might consider remaining in the Commonwealth with the
British Crown as the ceremonial head, Nehru's response was immediate and firm: "We will be
nobody's dominion. We will be a free republic or nothing at all."
The concept of sovereignty was tested early in India's history. During the 1962 war with China,
many questioned whether a newly independent nation could defend its sovereignty. The
nation's response—unity across party lines, sacrifice by ordinary citizens, and determination to
protect our borders—proved that sovereignty wasn't just a word in the Preamble but a lived
reality.
This power is absolute and supreme. India can enter into treaties, make its own laws, defend
its borders, and manage its affairs without interference. Sovereignty is what makes India a truly
free nation on the map of the world.
Socialist and Secular – India’s Unique Identity
The addition of "Socialist" in 1976 sparked debates that continue today. What did the framers
mean by socialism? Was India adopting the Soviet model? The answer is nuanced and
fascinating.
Indian socialism was never about government control over all means of production. Instead, it
represented a commitment to reducing inequality and ensuring that economic growth benefited
all sections of society. Nehru called it "a third way"—neither the unbridled capitalism of the
West nor the authoritarian communism of the East.
Socialism here doesn’t mean the government controls everything but that the Indian state works
to ensure no citizen is left behind — whether poor or rich, everyone should have equal access
to opportunities and resources.
Consider this remarkable example: India's Public Distribution System, which provides
subsidized food to millions, is a direct implementation of the socialist principle in the Preamble.
When the COVID-19 pandemic struck, this system became a lifeline for 800 million people—
the largest food security program in human history.
Then, Secular means India respects all religions equally. The word "secular" in the Indian
context doesn't mean anti-religious—it means equal respect for all religions. This was a
revolutionary concept in a world where most nations had official religions or were explicitly
atheistic.
The genius of Indian secularism lies in its inclusive approach. Unlike French secularism, which
keeps religion out of public life, Indian secularism allows all religions to flourish while
ensuring none dominates the state apparatus.
Here's a beautiful example: During Diwali, the President of India lights lamps at Rashtrapati
Bhavan. During Eid, the same President hosts an Iftar party. During Christmas, there's a
celebration at the highest levels of government. During Guru Nanak's birthday, the President
visits gurdwaras. This isn't tokenism—it's the living embodiment of constitutional secularism.
In a world filled with religious conflict, India made a bold choice — no religion would be
given preference by the state. Whether Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Sikh, or any other, all are
free to practice their beliefs peacefully. This secures harmony and respect for diversity in this
multi-faith nation.
The word Democratic means government by the people — a system where citizens exercise
their right to vote and choose leaders who represent their interests. Democracy is the heart of
India’s political system, making it one of the largest democracies in the world. The combination
of "Democratic Republic" represents a careful balance. Democratic ensures that power flows
from the people through elections. Republic ensures that the head of state is chosen by merit
and election, not by birth.
This principle was tested dramatically in 1977 when Indira Gandhi, despite being the Prime
Minister, lost the general election following the Emergency. The peaceful transfer of power
demonstrated that Indian democracy was robust enough to remove even its most powerful
leaders through the ballot box.
Republic means that the head of the country is elected and not born into power like a king or
queen. The President of India is chosen by representatives of the people, ensuring leadership
reflects the will of the nation. Together, these words promise a government accountable to the
people, working for their welfare, and respecting their rights.
Moving forward words like Justice, Liberty, Equality, Fraternity are the Core Values.
These four powerful words are called the heart and soul of the Preamble: Lets break it down-
 Justice: India promises fairness to all — not only in courts but in social and economic
life. Justice means fighting discrimination, poverty, and exploitation, so everyone has
a fair chance to succeed. When the Preamble promises "Justice, social, economic and
political," it's making a commitment that goes far beyond legal proceedings. It's
promising a society where fairness permeates every aspect of life. When thousands died
in the Bhopal Gas Tragedy in 1984, it was the Constitution's promise of justice that led
to landmark legal reforms in environmental protection and corporate accountability. As
they say, "Justice delayed is justice denied" - and this principle continues to drive India's
judicial reforms.
 Liberty: This is more than freedom from oppression. It includes freedom of thought,
speech, belief, faith, and worship. Without liberty, democracy cannot survive. Right
now, India's media can criticize the government, report freely, and hold power
accountable. This is the liberty promised in the Preamble at work. We have over
100,000 newspapers and magazines in more than 20 languages - one of the most diverse
media landscapes in the world!
 Equality: India refuses discrimination based on caste, creed, religion, gender, or
economic status. Every citizen must be treated equally before the law and in society.in
everyday life: The reservation system in education and jobs - whether you support it or
debate it - is a direct attempt to fulfill the Preamble's promise of equality of opportunity.
As Dr. B.R. Ambedkar said: "I measure the progress of a community by the degree of
progress which women have achieved."
 Fraternity: This is the idea of brotherhood and unity, encouraging citizens to live in
harmony, respecting one another’s differences, and working for the common
good.Across differences: During natural disasters, Indians help each other regardless of
religion, caste, or region. During COVID-19, Sikh communities served free meals to
people of all faiths, and various religious organizations opened their doors as quarantine
centers. This is fraternity in action!
Together, these values build a strong, inclusive society.
Now you might wonder - can you actually go to court if someone violates the Preamble? Here's

of the most significant rulings in the history of Indian constitutional law. Decided in 1973, this

While you can't directly file a case saying "my Preamble rights are violated," the Preamble
forms the basic structure of the Constitution and guides the interpretation of all laws. What
does this mean for you? Every law, every judgment, every government policy must align with
the values in the Preamble.

Longest Written Constitution
Let’s start with something that makes India’s Constitution stand out instantly — its
sheer size. When we say ‘lengthiest written Constitution’, we are not talking about
a few extra pages. We are talking about the world record holder in the Constitution

framework for local self-government into it. That’s how today every village has a
constitutionally recognised gram panchayat — not just a policy, but a guarantee in

The second feature is a Blend of Rigidity and Flexibility
Okay, so we have seen that the Constitution is long. But here’s the next cool part:
it’s not a stiff old rulebook locked in a high shelf for ages. It’s more like the
Operating System of a phone where some core functions are locked so you don’t
brick the device, but you can still customise the look, add apps and update features.
Hence, some provisions are rigid. They can only be changed by a special majority
in Parliament plus ratification by at least half the states. These are the nonnegotiables — like the kernel of an operating system.
While other provisions are flexible. They can be altered by a simple majority in
Parliament. This lets lawmakers tweak everyday matters without disturbing the core

Think of it as your phone: you can change ringtones and themes which are flexible,

On the other hand, Parliament frequently passes routine laws — like changing the
name of a state or reorganising a ministry — with a simple majority, showing the
‘flexible’ side.
So, this blend locks in the Constitution’s core principles but still lets India update
and innovate without a reboot. That’s why it’s stayed relevant for over seven
decades!
Feature 3: Federal System with a Unitary Bias
India calls itself a ‘Union of States.’ But what does that actually look like? Imagine
a group project in your college: everyone has their own section to do, but there’s one
team lead who can step in if the project’s about to fall apart. That’s basically how
our Constitution sets up federalism.
We have a federal structure — powers are divided between the Centre and the States
through Union, State and Concurrent Lists. States handle police, health and local
matters; the Centre handles defence, foreign policy and national finance.
But our Constitution also gives the Centre the ability to step in under special
circumstances — emergencies, constitutional breakdowns in a state, or when
national unity is at stake. That’s the unitary bias.
It’s like a co-op video game: each player or the states has their own controller, but
Player 1 or the Centre can take over when there’s a boss battle to keep the team alive.
A clear illustration is the 1975–77 National Emergency. During the Emergency
declared by Indira Gandhi under Article 352, most powers shifted from the states to
the Centre. Fundamental Rights were suspended and state governments functioned
effectively under central direction. This is the clearest example of the Constitution’s
unitary tilt in a crisis.
Feature 4: Parliamentary Form of Government
India’s Constitution picked the Parliamentary system — think of it as the original
checks-and-balances app. It is designed so no one in power can ghost the people.
In this system the executive grows out of the legislature. The President is the formal
head of state, but the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers run the show — and
they can only do so as long as they have the confidence of the Lok Sabha. Lose that
confidence? You’re out.
In 1999, Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee’s government fell by just one vote in
a confidence motion. That’s the Parliamentary system working exactly as intended
— leadership can change without a full-blown constitutional crisis.
This setup keeps the executive constantly answerable to Parliament, and by
extension, to the people. Flexible, accountable, and democratic — that’s the heart of
our Parliamentary form of government
Feature 5 – Rule of Law & Fundamental Rights
Ever tried playing a game where only some players must follow the rules while
others don’t? Total chaos, right? That’s exactly what the Rule of Law prevents in
India.
Our Constitution says no one is above the law — not ministers, not celebrities, not
even the government itself. This principle anchors the entire legal system.
And to make that real for citizens, it enshrines Fundamental Rights — freedom of
speech, equality before the law, protection of life and liberty, and more. These are
not gifts from the state; they are guarantees the state must respect.
In the landmark Keshavananda Bharati case of 1973, the Supreme Court held that
even Parliament cannot alter the Constitution’s ‘basic structure,’ which includes
Fundamental Rights and the Rule of Law. This decision put a firm leash on state
power.
So the Rule of Law plus Fundamental Rights is like India’s constitutional firewall
— keeping government power in check and protecting your core freedoms every
day.
Feature 6 – Secularism
India isn’t just multilingual or multicultural — it’s also multi-faith on a massive
scale. The Constitution’s answer? Secularism — our official ‘no-favouritism’
policy.
In Indian secularism, the state doesn’t run on one religion’s playbook. Instead, it
respects all faiths equally and keeps a principled distance. That means no special
perks for one, no discrimination against another. Citizens are free to practice,
profess, and propagate any religion — or none at all
In the S.R. Bommai case of 1994, the Supreme Court held that secularism is part of
the Constitution’s basic structure, and governments acting in an overtly communal
way can even be dismissed. That’s secularism with legal teeth.
So, secularism in India isn’t about deleting religion — it’s about ensuring every
citizen’s belief (or non-belief) coexists under one neutral state umbrella. Equal
respect, equal freedom.
Feature 7 – Fundamental Duties
We have established that rights are awesome! But imagine a game where everyone
just grabs loot and never helps the team. That’s where Fundamental Duties come in.
Added by the 42nd Amendment in 1976, Fundamental Duties remind citizens of
their responsibilities alongside their rights. Respect the Constitution, cherish the
national flag, protect the environment, promote harmony — these aren’t just
suggestions; they set the tone for responsible citizenship.
In M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, the Supreme Court often cited citizens’ duty to
protect the environment to enforce laws against pollution. Here, duties became
actionable — showing that rights + responsibilities = better governance.
So, Fundamental Duties are like the ‘side quests’ in India’s democracy: they may
not be mandatory in court, but completing them keeps the game — our nation —
fair, strong, and sustainable for everyone.
Feature 8- Independent Judiciary
Think of the judiciary as the ultimate referee in India’s democracy — no red cards

Our Constitution ensures an independent judiciary, free from executive or legislative
interference. This means judges can uphold the law and protect citizens’ rights
without pressure, keeping the balance of power intact. Judicial review is their
superpower — they can strike down laws or actions that violate the Constitution.
In the Indira Gandhi vs. Raj Narain case of 1975, the Supreme Court struck down
amendments that tried to curtail judicial review, reinforcing that even the Prime
Minister is not above the Constitution. This shows the judiciary can act as a check
on all branches of government.
So, an independent judiciary is like the ultimate watchdog and referee rolled into

Sources of Indian Constitution
The Constitution of India is the backbone of democracy in our country. It is an
umbrella of rights that gives the citizens an assurance of a free and fair society.
The Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26th November 1949 and
it came into effect on 26th of January 1950.
Sources:
The sources of the Indian Constitution are a blend of the Government of India Act,
1935, which provided the structural framework, and various other constitutions
from around the world. Key international influences include the British
Constitution for its parliamentary system, the US Constitution for fundamental
rights and judicial review, the Irish Constitution for the Directive Principles of
State Policy, and the Canadian Constitution for a strong federal center. Other
sources include the Australian Constitution, the Weimar Constitution of Germany,
and the Soviet Union (USSR) Constitution.
Government of India Act 1935
The Constitution of 1950 was a by-product of the legacy started by the
Government of India Act 1935. This was the longest act passed by the British
government with 321 sections and 10 schedules. This act had drawn its content
from four sources – Report of the Simon Commission, discussions and
deliberations at the Third Round Table Conference, the White Paper of 1933 and
the reports of the Joint select committees.
This act abolished the system of provincial dyarchy and suggested the
establishment of dyarchy at the centre and a ‘Federation of India’ consisting of the
provinces of British India and most of the princely states.
Most importantly, the act established the office of the Governor; all the executive
powers and authority of the centre was vested in the Governor.
Some features of the Government of India Act 1935 were:
● Federal scheme: Division of powers between the center and states.
Federal Legislature: The act suggested that the legislature will have two houses,
i.e., the Council of States and a Federal Assembly. The Council of States was the
upper house which was a permanent body with a tenure of three years and
composed of 260 members of which 156 were representatives of British India and
101 of the Princely Indian states. The Federal Assembly was the lower house
with a tenure expanding up to five years and its composition included 250
representatives of British India and 125 members from Princely states.
■ Provincial Autonomy: This act enabled the Provincial Governments to
be responsible only to Provincial Legislatures and helped them break
free from external control and intrusion. It was with the establishment
of this act that the powers between the centre and provinces were
divided in terms of three lists – Federal list (59 items for the Centre),
Provincial list (54 items for Provinces) and Concurrent list (36 items for
both). The Residuary powers were handed over to the Viceroy.
Other Features borrowed from this Act were -
● Office of the Governor: Provisions for the office of the governor.
● Public Service Commissions: Establishment of public service commissions.
● Emergency provisions: Provisions for emergency situations.
● Judiciary: The structure and powers of the judiciary.
British Constitution
A lot of concepts and features of the Indian Constitution have its roots in Great
Britain. Some of those are:
■ Parliamentary form of government: In such form of government, the
country is governed by a cabinet of ministers led by the Prime Minister.
The Prime Minister is the head of the government whereas the President
i.e. the nominal head, is the head of the state. The main feature of the
parliamentary form of government is the availability of one or more
opposition parties that exists to keep a check on the ruling party and its
functioning.
■ Rule of Law: This basically states that a State is not governed either by
the representatives or by the people but only by the law of that country.
The concept of rule of law states that everyone is equal before the law;
even the ones making it. Article 14 of the Indian Constitution codifies
the rule of law.
Article 14: Right to Equality; The State shall not deny to any person equality
before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India.
■ The idea of a single citizenship: This implies that a person born or
migrated to Indian Territory can enjoy the political and civil rights of
India alone and no other country at the same time. Therefore, India does
not allow dual citizenship. Indian state also does not recognize state
citizenship implying that there should not be any demarcation made
between the citizens of two or more states within the territory of India.
■ Writs: The Supreme Court and High Courts in India has the power to
issue writs in order to make the Right to Constitutional Remedies
[Article 32 to 35] available to the citizens. There are five writs – Habeas
Corpus (produce the detained person before the court and release him if
detention is found illegal),Mandamus (an order from the Supreme Court
or the High Court to a lower court to perform public duty), Certiorari
(SC or HC issues the writ for quashing the order already passed by an
inferior court), Prohibition (issued by the SC or the HC to a lower court
to stop the latter from continuing with the procedures) and
Quo-Warranto (restrains a person from holding a public office he is not
entitled to hold). The Indian Constitution provides for these writs in
Articles 32 and 226.
Article 32 (1): The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings
for the enforcement of the rights conferred by this Part is guaranteed.
Article 32 (2): The Supreme Court shall have power to issue directions or orders or
writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo
warranto and certiorari, whichever may be appropriate, for the enforcement of any
of the rights conferred by this Part.
Article 226 (1): Notwithstanding anything in Article 32 every High Court shall
have power, throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction,
to issue to any person or authority, including in appropriate cases, any
Government, within those territories directions, orders or writs, including writs in
the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari,
or any of them, for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by Part III and
for any other purpose.
● Legislative procedure: The process of making laws.
● Cabinet system: The structure and function of the cabinet.
● Bicameralism: A two-house parliament.
● Parliamentary privileges: Special rights and immunities of parliament
members.
US Constitution
Some of the features borrowed from the USA are:
Fundamental Rights: Articles 12 to 32 of the Indian Constitution contains all the
fundamental rights. Fundamental rights are the basic human rights given to the
citizens of the country to assure them an equal stance in society. The six
fundamental rights are – Right to Equality, Right to Freedom, Right against
Exploitation, Right to Freedom of Religion, Cultural and Educational Rights
and Right to Constitutional Remedies.
Judicial Review: The provision of Judicial Review gives the judiciary an upper
hand in interpreting the Constitution. The judiciary can thus nullify any order by
the legislature or executive if that order is in conflict with the Constitution of the
country.
The Basic Structure Doctrine, is an Indian judicial principle asserting that the
power of the Parliament to amend the Constitution is limited by the Constitution.
■ It means that the Constitution has several basic features that cannot be
amended.
■ This applies only to Constitutional amendments and not to ordinary acts
of the Parliament.
■ The initial stand of the Supreme court, that any part of the Constitution
is amendable, whilst in compliance with the article 368, including the
Fundamental Rights and the Article 368 was first challenged by Justice
JR Madholkar in the year 1964, in his dissent of the Sajjan Singh vs
State of Rajasthan judgement.
■ The Kesavananda Bharati vs State of Kerala proceedings (1973), a
landmark ruling, answered one main question: Was the power of the
Parliament to amend any part of the Constitution unlimited? This
judgement ruled that the Constitution cannot be amended so as to affect
the basic structure, in contrast to the earlier judgement in the 1967
Golaknath case, which had concluded that the Parliament cannot amend
so as to take away the Fundamental Right of a citizen.
■ Indira Gandhi violated the Doctrine with Emergency in 1975 and tried
to prevent her prosecution by the 39th amendment . Chief Justice Ray
attempted to review the Kesavananda Bharati judgement by calling a
bench of 13 Judges, including himself, but within two days, he was
narrowed down by a majority of 12:1; and the 39th and 41st amendment
were struck down.
Impeachment of the President and Removal of Judges: Article 61 of the Indian
Constitution provides for the impeachment of the President through legislative
procedures carried out by the two houses of the Parliament. Article 124 (4) of the
Indian Constitution and the provisions of the Judges Inquiry Act of 1968 deal
with the removal of judges.
Article 124 (4): A Judge of the Supreme Court shall not be removed from his of ice
except by an order of the President passed after an address by each House of
Parliament supported by a majority of the total membership of that House and by a
majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and
voting has been presented to the President in the same session for such removal on
the ground of proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

Removal of Supreme Court and High Court judges: The procedure for removing

Irish Constitution
The main feature borrowed from the Irish Constitution is the provision of the
Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP). The DPSP are listed in the Part IV of
the Indian Constitution and it clearly states that it is the duty of the State to apply
these principles in the process of law making. There are mainly three categories of
these principles – Socialist Directives, Gandhian Directives and Liberal Intellectual
Directives. The procedure for the nomination of members to the Rajya Sabha is
also borrowed from Ireland.
Method of election of the President: The procedure for electing the president.
Canadian Constitution
The provisions of a Federation with a strong centre, Residuary powers of the
Centre, appointment of State governors by the Centre and the advisory jurisdiction
of the Supreme Court, have all been borrowed from the Canadian constitution.
Article 248 of the Indian Constitution states that the Parliament has the sole power
to make laws regarding any item not mentioned in the Union and State lists
respectively. Article 143 provides for an advisory jurisdiction for the Supreme
Court. Under this provision, the President may seek opinion of the Supreme Court
on public matters and the Supreme Court may then further give its opinion after
studying the case properly. Appointment of governors: The appointment of state
governors by the central government. Advisory jurisdiction of the Supreme Court:
The power of the Supreme Court to give advice on matters of law.
France
The Indian Preamble borrowed its ideals of Liberty, Equality and Fraternity from
the French Constitution. The Indian state came to be recognized as the ‘Republic of
India’ in the lineage of the Constitution of France.
Australia
The Constitution of Australia lent us the provisions of Freedom of Trade and
Commerce within the country and between the states. The provisions of the same
are laid down in the Articles 301-307 of the Indian Constitution. We also received
the provisions of the Concurrent list and the joint sitting of both the houses of
Parliament from Australia.
South Africa and Germany
While the Constitution of South Africa gave us the provisions of the procedure of
the amendment and the Election of the Rajya Sabha members, the German
Constitution, gave us the provision of suspension of fundamental rights during
emergency.
These were the major sources of the Indian Constitution. As the father of our
Constitution and the Chairman of the Drafting Committee, Dr. B.R. Ambedkar
said, “As to the accusation that the Draft Constitution has [re]produced a good part
of the provisions of the Government of India Act, 1935, I make no apologies.
There is nothing to be ashamed of in borrowing. It involves no plagiarism. Nobody
holds any patent rights in the fundamental ideas of a Constitution….”
Other notable sources
● Soviet Union (USSR) Constitution: Fundamental duties of citizens and the
ideals of social, economic, and political justice in the preamble.
● Japanese Constitution: Procedure established by law.

Week - 2
Philosophical Underpinnings of Indian Constitution
Hello and welcome back learners! Did you know? That the Indian Constitution is often described
not merely as a legal or political document but as a profoundly philosophical text.
Let's Decode !
Granville Austin, in his classical work The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation (1966),
reminds us that the Constitution embodies the "aspirations of people emerging from colonial
rule into freedom." But these aspirations were not abstract—they were deeply rooted in
philosophical traditions, debates, and contestations that defined India’s intellectual and political
landscape in the early twentieth century. Welcome to our exploration of something truly
remarkable - the philosophy that breathes life into the Indian Constitution. You might wonder:
why does a legal document need philosophy? Isn't law just about rules and regulations?
Well! When the framers sat in the Constituent Assembly, they weren't just drafting laws. They
were answering fundamental questions: What kind of nation do we want to be? How do we
balance individual freedom with social justice? How do we unite a diverse population while
respecting their differences? Here's what makes our Constitution unique: it wasn't designed just
to limit government power - though it does that brilliantly. It was conceived as an instrument of
social transformation. Nehru captured this beautifully when he described the Constituent
Assembly as "a nation on the move, throwing away the shell of its past political and possibly
social structure."
This transformative vision meant the Constitution had to do something unprecedented: empower
those who had been traditionally powerless while creating a framework for democratic
governance. It had to break age-old hierarchies while building new institutions.
A political philosophical approach to the constitution is needed not only to find out the moral
content expressed in it and to evaluate its claims but possibly to use it to arbitrate between
varying interpretations of the many core values in our polity. It is obvious that many of its ideals
are challenged, discussed, debated and contested in different political arenas, in the legislatures,
in party forums, in the press, in schools and universities. These ideals are variously interpreted
and sometimes wilfully manipulated to suit partisan short term interests. We must, therefore,
examine whether or not a serious disjunction exists between the constitutional ideal and its
expression in other arenas. Sometimes, the same ideal is interpreted differently by different
institutions.
Lets discuss- The Six Pillars of Constitutional Philosophy
First - Individual Freedom:
The Constitution's commitment to individual freedom didn't emerge overnight. It was forged
through decades of struggle against colonial oppression. When Raja Ram Mohan Roy fought for
press freedom in the early 1800s, when Indians protested the infamous Rowlatt Act that allowed
arbitrary arrests, they were laying the groundwork for the freedoms we enjoy today.
Our fundamental rights - freedom of speech, freedom from arbitrary arrest, freedom of
conscience - these aren't abstract concepts. They're responses to specific historical injustices.
Every freedom in our Constitution has a story of struggle behind it.
Second- Social Justice:
Here's where our Constitution gets really interesting. While Western liberal constitutions
typically focus on individual rights, ours goes further. It recognizes that merely declaring
equality isn't enough when centuries of oppression have created deep inequalities.
Take reservations for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes for instance, Critics call it reverse
discrimination, but the Constitution's philosophy is clear: formal equality without substantive
justice is meaningless. If you've been pushed down for centuries, just removing the barriers isn't
enough - you need a helping hand to climb up.
This is what makes Indian liberalism unique. It doesn't just protect individual rights; it actively
works to create conditions where those rights can be meaningfully exercised by everyone.
Third- Unity in Diversity:
One of the trickiest challenges our Constitution faced was how to deal with community
identities. Should we ignore communities entirely, as many Western constitutions do? Or should
we recognize and protect them?
The framers chose a middle path that was both pragmatic and principled. They recognized that in
a diverse country like India, community identities matter deeply to people's sense of self-respect
and dignity. At the same time, they ensured no single community could dominate others.
This is why we have provisions like minority rights to establish educational institutions, or
special status for certain regions like Nagaland under Article 371A. These aren't departures from
equality - they're applications of unity in a diverse society.
Fourth is . Secularism:
When we talk about secularism, most people think of the wall of separation between religion and
state. But Indian secularism is more nuanced and, arguably, more practical.
Instead of complete separation, we practice what scholars call "principled distance." The state
maintains equal distance from all religions, but this doesn't mean it never intervenes in religious
matters. When religious practices violate human dignity - like untouchability - the state must
intervene. When religious communities need support for education, the state can provide it.
This flexible approach allows us to protect religious freedom while ensuring that religion doesn't
become a tool for oppression or discrimination.
Fifth is . Universal Franchise:
In 1950, giving every adult citizen the right to vote was revolutionary. Remember, in many
Western democracies, women and working-class people had only recently gained voting rights.
Some critics worried that illiterate masses couldn't make informed decisions.
But our Constitution makers had faith in the common person. They believed that political
participation was both a right and a tool for empowerment. Universal franchise wasn't just about
democracy; it was about dignity - recognizing that every individual, regardless of education or
wealth, has a stake in the nation's future.
Last but not the least Federalism:
Our federal structure might seem contradictory - we have a strong center but also significant
state autonomy. And then there are special provisions for different states and regions. This isn't
confusion;- it is a sophisticated constitutional design.
The framers understood that India's diversity requires flexibility. What works in Punjab might
not work in Nagaland. What Kerala needs might be different from what Rajasthan needs.
Asymmetric federalism - giving different states different arrangements based on their specific
needs - allows for unity without uniformity.
The philosophical foundations of the Constitution have also emerged from a combination of
modern Western political thought and indigenous Indian traditions of justice, morality, and
governance. This duality—between universal ideals and rooted cultural values—is what gives
the Indian Constitution its distinctive character.
Moving ahead
The Indian Constitution did not emerge in isolation. It was the culmination of centuries of
intellectual churn, but most directly it drew from the philosophical ferment of the Indian freedom
movement. As political theorist Rajeev Bhargava notes in Politics and Ethics of the Indian
Constitution (2008), the Constitution is "the outcome of long deliberations about the moral
foundations of independent India." The freedom struggle, therefore, was not only about resisting
colonial rule—it was equally about reimagining what Indian society could and should become.
This reimagining brought together diverse philosophical visions, often in dialogue, sometimes in
tension, but always directed toward building a just and inclusive society.
The constitution reflects Gandhi’s Moral Philosophy: Swaraj and Ethics in Politics
Mahatma Gandhi was arguably the most distinctive moral voice of the movement. In Hind
Swaraj (1909), Gandhi argued that "real home-rule is self-rule or self-control." His conception of
Swaraj was not merely about expelling the British—it was about cultivating inner discipline,
ethical self-mastery, and non-violence (ahimsa) as the foundation of political life.
Consider, for example, the Salt March of 1930. At one level, it was a protest against a colonial
tax on salt, but at a deeper level, it symbolized Gandhi’s conviction that politics must be ethical
and accessible to the poorest. Even though Gandhi’s village-centered vision of gram swaraj was
not fully realized in the constitutional framework, his emphasis on human dignity, non-violence,
and duties continues to echo in our constitutional culture. Fundamental Duties (Article 51A) and
the moral undertone of non-violence in Indian politics can be traced back to Gandhian
philosophy.
Ambedkar’s Philosophy of Justice: Equality, Liberty, Fraternity
If Gandhi brought ethics into politics, B.R. Ambedkar brought justice to its very core. In his
Annihilation of Caste (1936), Ambedkar insisted that "justice has always evoked ideas of
equality. Rules of justice require that all men shall be treated as equals." He envisioned
constitutional democracy not as mere procedure but as a moral commitment to dismantling
hierarchies of caste and privilege.
Ambedkar’s intellectual grounding drew from Western liberal traditions, the French Revolution,
and his deep engagement with Buddhism. Yet, it was also rooted in the lived experience of
untouchability and exclusion. His insistence on reservations for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled
Tribes was not charity but a corrective mechanism for centuries of structural oppression.
The Preamble’s ringing call to "Justice—social, economic, and political" reflects Ambedkar’s
vision. Moreover, his invocation of fraternity as a binding principle was not just an abstract ideal;
it was a plea to see beyond caste, creed, and religion in the everyday practice of citizenship.
Nehru’s Modernist Vision: Scientific Temper and Development
Jawaharlal Nehru, India’s first Prime Minister, embodied another current of thought: modernity.
For Nehru, independence meant not just political freedom but entry into a new age of rationality,
industrial progress, and social reform. In The Discovery of India (1946), he wrote: "A new star
rises, the star of freedom in the East." That star, for him, was inseparable from scientific inquiry,
technological advancement, and planned economic growth.
This is why Nehru championed the establishment of institutions such as the Indian Institutes of
Technology and the Planning Commission. His philosophical influence on the Constitution is
visible in the Directive Principles of State Policy, which reflect goals of welfare,
industrialization, and equal opportunity. The constitutional commitment to promote scientific
temper (later reinforced in Article 51A) is a clear echo of Nehru’s worldview.
Tagore’s Humanism: Unity in Diversity
Rabindranath Tagore, though not directly involved in constitution-making, offered a profound
philosophical lens that influenced the freedom movement. In his lectures compiled as
Nationalism (1917), Tagore warned against the dangers of narrow, exclusivist nationalism. He
instead emphasized a spiritual cosmopolitanism rooted in India’s civilizational ethos.
For Tagore, "India has ever declared that unity is truth, diversity is God’s manifestation." This
profound insight resonates with the constitutional value of secularism, which recognizes and
respects India’s plurality of religions, languages, and cultures. The idea of fraternity, too, carries
shades of Tagorean humanism, reminding citizens that unity is not uniformity but harmony amid
difference.
Tagore’s school at Santiniketan, with its blending of Indian and global traditions, was a lived
experiment in this philosophy of inclusiveness. The Constitution, in its secular fabric and
recognition of minority rights, carries forward that vision.
Western Philosophical Influences -
The Constituent Assembly debates reveal the deep imprint of Western political thought. But
these were not wholesale borrowings; rather, they were adapted and indigenized to suit Indian
realities.
● Liberalism: Thinkers like John Locke, Montesquieu, and J.S. Mill influenced the idea of
limited government, separation of powers, and the primacy of individual rights. Locke’s
notion of natural rights—life, liberty, and property—inspired the framing of Fundamental
Rights. Montesquieu’s theory of separation of powers shaped the institutional design of
the legislature, executive, and judiciary.
● Republicanism and Democracy: From Rousseau came the idea of popular
sovereignty—the notion that political authority rests with the people. The American Bill
of Rights provided a model for justiciable fundamental rights.
● Socialism and Economic Justice: The Russian Revolution and Fabian socialism
influenced Indian leaders like Nehru and Ambedkar, embedding in the Constitution a
commitment to reduce inequality. The Directive Principles of State Policy reflect this
socialist orientation, advocating redistribution, welfare, and social justice.
● Humanism and Universalism: The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)
provided a global moral context in which the Indian Constitution was drafted. The
emphasis on dignity, equality, and freedom resonates with these global currents.
Upendra Baxi has rightly argued that, the Constitution is a "social document" that mediates
between the liberal rights-based order and the socialist aspirations of a newly independent
society (The Indian Supreme Court and Politics, 1980).
While Western influences are undeniable, the philosophical underpinnings of the Constitution
cannot be understood without Indian traditions. As Rajeev Dhavan notes, "Constitutionalism in
India is not simply Western modernity grafted onto Indian soil; it is a creative synthesis with
indigenous values" (The Supreme Court of India: A Socio-Legal Critique, 1977).
● Dharma: The ancient Indian concept of Dharma emphasized moral order, justice, and
duties. The Manusmriti, the Arthashastra, and Buddhist texts all highlight governance as
rooted in ethics. The Constitution’s emphasis on duties, justice, and moral responsibility
reflects this heritage.
● Pluralism and Tolerance: India’s civilizational ethos, shaped by Hindu, Buddhist, Jain,
Islamic, and Sikh traditions, emphasized religious coexistence. Akbar’s philosophy of
Sulh-i-Kul (peace with all) and Ashoka’s edicts of tolerance resonate with the
constitutional principle of secularism.
● Bhakti and Sufi Movements: These movements challenged rigid hierarchies and
promoted egalitarianism and fraternity. Their spiritual democracy finds echoes in the
constitutional principle of fraternity.
● Gandhian Traditions: Gandhi drew upon Indian ethical traditions—ahimsa
(non-violence), sarvodaya (welfare of all), and swaraj (self-rule). Though his
village-centered economic vision was sidelined, his moral philosophy influenced
constitutional values of peace, tolerance, and justice.
Thus, the Indian Constitution rests upon a dialogue between Dharma and Democracy, between
Indian traditions of moral responsibility and modern ideals of individual rights.
Justice, Liberty, Equality, and Fraternity as Philosophical Ideals
Dr. Ambedkar, while introducing the Draft Constitution in the Assembly, famously said that
these principles of justice, liberty, equality, and fraternity form the bedrock of the constitutional
order.
● Justice: Beyond legal justice, the Constitution emphasizes social and economic justice.
This is seen in the abolition of untouchability (Article 17), provisions for affirmative
action, and the Directive Principles.
● Liberty: Not merely freedom from state control but also positive freedoms—freedom of
thought, expression, belief, and association.
● Equality: Equality before law (Article 14) is coupled with provisions for substantive
equality, such as reservations for Scheduled Castes and Tribes.
● Fraternity: Perhaps the most overlooked, fraternity emphasizes the dignity of the
individual and the unity of the nation. It is a moral and emotional glue, essential for
democracy’s survival.
As political philosopher Niraja Gopal Jayal in her Citizenship and Its Discontents, 2013) rightly
argues, fraternity is the "forgotten ideal" of the Constitution, yet without it, liberty and equality
cannot be sustained.
Let's talk about Procedural Achievements: How We Make
Decisions
The Constitution's philosophy isn't just about what we believe; it's about how we arrive at those
beliefs. Two procedural achievements stand out:
Faith in Deliberation: The Constituent Assembly debates show a remarkable commitment to
reasoned discussion. Even when disagreements were sharp, members tried to convince each
other through arguments, not force or manipulation.
Spirit of Compromise: In a diverse democracy, pure positions rarely work. The Constitution
embodies the wisdom of principled compromise - trading off some of one value to secure
another, but always through open debate among equals.
A Dialogue of Philosophies
It is important to recognize that these philosophical influences were not always in harmony.
Gandhi’s emphasis on village life stood in contrast to Nehru’s faith in industrialization.
Ambedkar’s suspicion of caste-based traditions clashed with Gandhi’s more accommodative
approach. Tagore’s universalism was wary of the very nationalism that Nehru embraced.
Yet, it was precisely this dialogue, this clash and negotiation of ideas, that enriched the
Constitution. The Constituent Assembly became the space where these philosophies converged.
Ambedkar himself acknowledged that the Constitution represented not a single doctrine but a
"workable compromise." This compromise, however, was not a weakness; it was the strength of
a plural democracy seeking to reconcile multiple aspirations.
So Why These Philosophical Roots Still Matter?
Today, when debates about secularism, social justice, or development resurface, they echo these
foundational philosophies. For instance:
● Movements for environmental sustainability often invoke Gandhi’s critique of industrial
excess.
● Affirmative action policies directly stem from Ambedkar’s insistence on equality.
● The push for innovation and scientific education reflects Nehruvian ideals.
● The defense of multiculturalism and pluralism draws on Tagore’s vision.
Thus, the Constitution is not a frozen text but a living document, continually animated by the
philosophies that shaped its birth.
Now Let's Address the Critics!
No constitution is perfect, and ours faces legitimate criticisms too. Some say it's too long and
unwieldy, but this comprehensiveness was deliberate - including detailed provisions for
institutions like the Election Commission directly in the constitutional text.
Others argue it's unrepresentative because the Constituent Assembly wasn't elected by universal
franchise. This criticism has merit regarding "voice" - people weren't directly represented. But in
terms of "opinion," the debates show that virtually every significant viewpoint in Indian society
was articulated and considered.
The charge that it's an "alien" document borrowed from the West misses the point entirely. Yes,
we borrowed extensively - from the British parliamentary system, American federalism, Irish
directive principles. But this was innovative borrowing, adaptation to Indian conditions, not
blind copying.
Therefore, it is quite fair enough to say that The Indian Constitution, then, was never meant to be
a mere rulebook.
It was, and remains, a philosophical dialogue between ethics, justice, modernity, and humanism.
Gandhi, Ambedkar, Nehru, and Tagore may have walked different paths, but their ideas
collectively ensured that India’s democracy would rest not only on laws but on moral and
philosophical foundations. To study these influences is to understand that the Constitution is not
just about governing the state—it is about imagining a good life for all citizens.
To conclude The Indian Constitution is therefore best understood as a philosophical project of
nation-building. It integrates Western liberalism and socialism with Indian traditions of Dharma
and pluralism. It embodies Gandhi’s ethics, Ambedkar’s justice, Nehru’s modernity, and Tagore’s
humanism. It is, in Ambedkar’s words, not just a political document but a "social revolution on
paper."

Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy
Welcome back, learners, once again, to this journey where we take you
through the Indian Constitution. My name is Amrita and today, we shall dive
into another important yet powerful content of the Indian constitution.
Now, I know what you’re thinking: “Constitution? That’s a boring book full
of laws”
But hold on!
Think of it less as an old, dusty textbook and more as the ultimate cheat
code for life in India. It’s the rulebook that tells you how to live freely,
safely, and justly, while keeping the chaos of 1.4 billion people from turning
the country into a real-life Game of Thrones scenario. Today, we’re diving
into two of its coolest features: Fundamental Rights and Directive
Principles of State Policy. If you’ve ever felt annoyed at unfairness,
frustrated by slow government action, or just wondered, “Do I even have
rights?”—this is your backstage pass to understanding exactly what the
Constitution does for you.
Let’s start with Fundamental Rights, the superhero suits you get just for
being an Indian citizen. Picture them as your Avengers team. Each right
has a distinct superpower designed to protect you from injustice,
discrimination, and random chaos.
Right to Equality is like your invisibility cloak in a world full of bias.
Remember this ridiculous rule under the colonial times when Indians could
not travel by the first class in trains or that there were signboards in public
places saying ‘Dogs and Indians are not allowed”? Infuriating, right? Right
to Equality, however, considers it illegal. It is like a protection that was
created so that such scenarios may never occur again in India.
Article 14 is the anchor: it declares that everyone is “equal before the law”
and gets “equal protection of the laws.” In simple terms, no VIP lanes when
it comes to rights and no discrimination by the State without a reasonable
basis — the same traffic rules apply to the PM’s car and to yours.
Article 15, which tells the State it cannot discriminate against you on
grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth in public spaces or
institutions. But it also recognises that some groups have been pushed
down for centuries, so it allows special provisions — like scholarships or
reservations — for women, children, and socially or educationally
backward classes to help level the field.
Article 16 takes that equality principle into the job market for government
employment. It guarantees equal opportunity in public employment and
bans discrimination on those same grounds, while again permitting
reservations for backward classes or for people from underrepresented
regions to ensure fair representation.
Article 17 is historic — it abolishes “untouchability” in any form. This isn’t
just a feel-good line; it makes practising untouchability a punishable
offence under law. It was a way of saying that centuries of caste-based
exclusion have no place in a democratic India.
Finally, Article 18 abolishes titles. That means the State can’t confer
hereditary or honorific titles like “Sir,” “Raja,” or “Maharaja” that create
artificial ranks among citizens. Awards like Padma Shri or Bharat Ratna are
honours but not titles you can pass down or demand special treatment for.
Together, Articles 14 to 18 are like a five-layer shield built into the
Constitution. However, equality, according to this right, isn’t just about
saying everyone is the same; it’s about creating opportunities for those
historically left behind. It’s like leveling up all players in a game so
everyone has a fair shot at winning. If some people start the race 10 steps
behind because of history, discrimination, or lack of resources, then giving
everyone the same slice of pizza still leaves the people at the back hungry.
Equality in the Indian Constitution isn’t just about formal equality (“the
same rules for all”); it’s about substantive equality — making sure
everyone actually has a fair shot at opportunities.
That’s why Article 15(4) and 16(4) allow the state to create special
provisions for socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled
Castes and Scheduled Tribes, women, and other groups who’ve been
historically disadvantaged. This is what we call reservations or affirmative
action. They’re not a “bonus” or a “favour”; they’re a way of recognising
that centuries of exclusion and discrimination can’t be undone simply by
declaring “from today we’re all equal.” You need active measures to close
the gap.
The second right,- Right to Freedom which is covered under article 19-22 is
your cheat code for life. Freedom of speech, expression, assembly, and
association—it’s all in here. Under Article 19, you get a whole package of
mini-freedoms: speech and expression, assembly, association, movement,
residence, and profession. In the digital age, that means you can tweet your
thoughts, start a podcast, upload a diss track, or post your art on
Instagram without the State arbitrarily pulling the plug. You can gather your
friends to hold a candlelight vigil, join a student union, or start a book club
on political memes. You can move from one state to another to study or
work, live where you choose, and pick your own career path.
You probably heard this phrase- innocent until proven guilty! That’s exactly
what article 20 is for! It’s there to protect you from being unfairly targeted
by the State when you’re accused of a crime. In simple terms, it lays down
three key shields:
First, no ex post facto laws — which basically means you can’t be punished
for something that wasn’t a crime when you did it !
Second, no double jeopardy — you can’t be tried or punished twice for the
same offence.
Third, no self-incrimination — you can’t be forced to testify against
yourself.
Together, these make article 20 one of the strongest “rule-of-law” clauses
in the Constitution. It tells the State: play fair, follow the rules, and don’t
stack the deck against the individual.
Article 21 of the Indian Constitution is like your ultimate
“right-to-live-your-life” guarantee. It says no one can take away your life or
personal liberty except according to a fair, established legal process. In
everyday terms, Article 21 makes sure that your life in India isn’t just
survival mode but a space where you can breathe, grow, and make your
own choices, as long as you respect the law.
Article 22 is like the “know your rights” section of the Constitution for
when you’re arrested or detained. It guarantees that if you’re picked up by
the police you have to be told why you’re being arrested, you have the right
to consult and be defended by a lawyer of your choice, and you must be
produced before a magistrate within 24 hours — no secret lock-ups beyond
that without judicial approval. At the same time, it recognises “preventive
detention,” which means the State can hold someone to stop an anticipated
threat to public order or national security, but even that comes with strict
time limits and review by advisory boards. In everyday language, Article 22
is the rule that says: if the State wants to take away your liberty, it has to
follow due process and can’t just whisk you away into a legal black hole.
Right to freedom is one of the strongest fundamental rights under which
you can tweet your opinions, start a club, or even organize a protest
without fear of arbitrary censorship. But like every cheat code, there are
limits. You can’t incite panic or threaten people. Freedom without
responsibility is chaos, just like wielding Thor’s hammer recklessly. But
like every truly unlimited plan, there’s an acceptable use policy built in.
Freedoms aren’t a free-for-all; they’re designed so that your liberty doesn’t
crush someone else’s. You can’t use “freedom of speech” as a shield for
hate speech, doxxing, or inciting violence online. You can assemble and
protest, but you can’t riot, damage property, or endanger others. You have
the freedom to travel and settle anywhere, but you can’t trespass on
restricted or protected areas. This isn’t about clipping your wings; it’s
about keeping the airspace safe so everyone can fly.
The third right- Right against Exploitation is guaranteed under article 23-24
of the constitution. It is like the Constitution’s “No Bullies Allowed” policy.
This right prohibits human trafficking, forced labor, and child labor. Article
24 specifically prohibits children under 14 from working in factories, mines
or any hazardous jobs. In real life terms, this is the Constitution saying:
you can’t treat people like machines or commodities, and kids belong in
schools and playgrounds, not sweatshops. It’s about protecting the most
vulnerable from being squeezed for profit or power. It’s the legal
recognition that every human being deserves dignity and fair treatment,
and that exploitation – whether physical, mental, or financial – is
unacceptable. In a world where gig work and informal labor are booming,
this right is a critical reminder that the state must step in when private
power tries to bend people to its will.
Right to Freedom of Religion, guaranteed under article 25-28,is particularly
special in a country as spiritually diverse as India. India’s Constitution
doesn’t pick a favourite religion; it’s like a giant food court where everyone
can choose their own meal or decide to fast. Article 25 gives every person
the freedom to profess, practise and propagate any religion of their choice,
subject to public order, health and morality. Articles 26 and 27 protect the
rights of religious groups to manage their own affairs and prevent the State
from forcing anyone to pay for promoting a religion they don’t follow.
Article 28 keeps government-funded schools free of compulsory religious
instruction. In short, you’re free to pray, not pray, or change your mind —
just don’t harm others in the process. This right allows you to practice,
propagate, and profess your religion freely. From Diwali to Eid, Christmas
to Guru Nanak Jayanti, it ensures that festivals, rituals, and beliefs can be
celebrated without fear. But again, there’s a balancing act here. Religious
freedom cannot be a license for social discrimination or violating the law.
In other words, you are free to believe, but you cannot harm others in the
name of belief.
The Cultural and Educational Rights are guaranteed under article 29-30.
These are the “preserve your playlist” rights for communities. Article 29
protects the right of any group of citizens to conserve their distinct
language, script or culture. Article 30 protect the interests of minorities by
allowing them to preserve their language, culture, and education. Think of it
as a constitutional guarantee that your traditions, your mother tongue, and
your heritage have a safe space in a rapidly modernizing world. This right
recognizes that identity is not just a personal choice; it’s a collective
inheritance that deserves protection.
Finally, the Right to Constitutional Remedies acts as the emergency exit in
case your rights are violated. This is the “ultimate help desk” of the
Constitution. If any of your Fundamental Rights are violated, you can go
straight to the Supreme Court or High Courts for relief. Article 32, often
called the heart of the Constitution by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar, empowers
citizens to approach the Supreme Court or High Courts directly to enforce
their rights. It’s a legal lifeline, ensuring that the shield of Fundamental
Rights isn’t just symbolic, but actionable.
If Fundamental Rights are like your personal superhero suit, giving you
protection and powers to live freely and safely. But a suit alone doesn’t
make the game fun or fair; you need rules for the world you’re playing in, a
map, a guide, and objectives. That’s what the Directive Principles of State
Policy (DPSPs) are. They don’t grant you personal powers the way
Fundamental Rights do, but they act as a moral compass and roadmap for
the government. They tell the state, “This is how you should organize
society, make policies, and improve citizens’ lives.”
DPSPs are enshrined in Part IV (Articles 36–51) of the Constitution. They
are not enforceable in courts—meaning you can’t drag the government to
court if it ignores them—but they carry political and moral weight. In other
words, they are the ultimate “good governance wishlist” for India. The
framers of the Constitution were visionaries. They had seen the exploitative
policies under British rule, systemic inequality, widespread poverty, and
lack of social justice. They realized that simply giving citizens rights wasn’t
enough if the state didn’t actively work to improve their lives.
The DPSPs can broadly be divided into social, economic, and political
principles, each with specific goals.
1. Social Justice and Welfare
At the heart of DPSPs is the vision of social justice. Articles like Article
39(a) and (b) direct the state to ensure that citizens have adequate means
of livelihood and that resources are distributed to prevent concentration of
wealth. In simpler terms, it’s a call to prevent society from becoming a
playground only for the rich. Imagine a cricket game where only a few
players have all the equipment and everyone else has broken bats; DPSPs
aim to level that field.
Education, healthcare, and nutrition also fall under this umbrella. Article 41
mandates the state to provide work, education, and public assistance in
cases of unemployment, old age, sickness, and disablement. Think of it as
the Constitution telling the government, “If people are struggling to
survive, give them a helping hand so they can participate fully in life.”
Programs like the Mid-Day Meal Scheme, public schools, and social
security pensions are modern expressions of these directives.
2. Economic Democracy
Economic democracy is about ensuring that wealth doesn’t get hoarded by
a few while the majority live in poverty. Articles 43 and 43A direct the state
to promote cottage industries, co-operative societies, and equal pay for
men and women. The idea is that economic power should be more evenly
distributed so that development benefits everyone, not just the elite. In
contemporary India, policies supporting microfinance, self-help groups,
and women entrepreneurs reflect this principle.
Similarly, Article 39(b) discourages the concentration of wealth and means
of production in a few hands, reminding us that true democracy is not just
political but also economic. DPSPs envision a society where the poor can
aspire, earn, and thrive without being blocked by systemic inequality.
3. Gandhian Principles
Several DPSPs reflect Mahatma Gandhi’s vision of a just and self-reliant
India. Articles like Article 40 call for the state to organize village
panchayats to enable self-governance. This isn’t just about giving people a
vote; it’s about empowering communities to make local decisions, solve
problems, and develop sustainably. Think of it like giving every village its
own “mini-game” where players actively shape outcomes rather than
waiting for a distant ruler.
Articles 43 and 48 emphasize agriculture, animal husbandry, and protection
of livestock and forests. Gandhi’s vision was of a self-reliant, rural India
where communities were economically independent, environmentally
conscious, and socially cohesive. These principles influence schemes like
MNREGA (employment guarantee for rural households), agricultural
subsidies, and forest conservation programs.
4. Environmental and Ecological Principles
Long before climate change became a global buzzword, DPSPs addressed
the importance of protecting natural resources. Article 48A, added through
the 42nd Amendment, directs the state to protect and improve the
environment and safeguard forests and wildlife. This reflects a
forward-thinking approach: sustainable development isn’t optional; it’s a
constitutional mandate. Programs for afforestation, pollution control,
renewable energy, and wildlife protection draw inspiration from these
directives.
5. Promotion of Education and Culture
Articles 45 and 51A stress that the state should provide free and
compulsory education for children and encourage research, learning, and
cultural development. Education isn’t just a skill; it’s a tool for
empowerment, reducing inequality, and fostering national integration.
Cultural preservation is equally important. India’s diversity is
immense—languages, scripts, art forms, and heritage need active
protection. DPSPs ensure that the government actively invests in both
education and cultural conservation, not just economic growth.
6. International Peace and Cooperation
Another unique feature of DPSPs is that they extend beyond domestic
governance. Article 51 urges India to promote international peace, maintain
just relations, and respect international law. The framers understood that
India doesn’t exist in isolation; a peaceful, cooperative global environment
is part of creating a secure, prosperous nation. India’s foreign aid
programs, participation in UN peacekeeping missions, and treaties
promoting disarmament are modern reflections of this principle.
Why DPSPs Matter Today
For young people, DPSPs may seem like distant ideals, but they shape
policies that affect your life every day. Scholarships, employment schemes,
public health initiatives, women’s empowerment programs, environmental
policies, and local self-governance—all trace back to these directives. They
are aspirational, yes, but they also guide politicians, bureaucrats, and
policymakers in the real world. They serve as benchmarks for progress: if
the state fails to implement these, citizens have the right to demand
accountability, even if not through courts directly.
Challenges and Criticisms
Of course, DPSPs aren’t perfect. Critics argue that because they’re not
enforceable, governments often ignore them, especially when short-term
political gains are at stake. Implementation gaps, corruption, and
bureaucratic inefficiency sometimes dilute their impact. But the brilliance
of DPSPs lies in their visionary character. They act as moral and ethical
compasses, reminding the state to prioritize welfare over narrow political or
economic interests. Even Supreme Court judgments have often invoked
DPSPs to interpret Fundamental Rights expansively, demonstrating their
indirect enforceability and continuing relevance.
DPSPs and Fundamental Rights: The Connection
It’s important to understand that DPSPs and Fundamental Rights are two
sides of the same coin. Rights protect you individually; directives guide the
state to create conditions where rights can be meaningfully exercised. For
example, your Right to Education becomes impactful only if schools exist,
teachers are trained, and mid-day meals are provided—all guided by
DPSPs. Your Right to Equality is supported by economic and social
directives that aim to reduce historical disadvantages. They complement
each other, ensuring that India’s democracy is both liberal and inclusive.
Youth Engagement and the Future
For citizens aged 16–28, DPSPs are more than legal text; they’re a call to
action. Understanding these principles equips you to engage critically with
policies, demand social justice, and participate in democratic processes.
Whether it’s volunteering in community programs, supporting renewable
energy initiatives, advocating for gender equality, or running educational
campaigns, youth are the most potent agents for turning these aspirational
principles into lived reality.
The Constitution envisioned DPSPs as living ideals. They evolve with time:
amendments, new policies, and judicial interpretations keep them relevant.
For instance, environmental concerns weren’t in the original DPSPs but
were added through the 42nd Amendment. Similarly, education evolved
from a directive to a Fundamental Right under Article 21A. This adaptability
makes DPSPs incredibly powerful—they are not just relics of 1949; they’re
the foundation for 21st-century governance.
Conclusion
In short, the Directive Principles of State Policy are the moral blueprint for
building an equitable, just, and progressive society. They aim for social
justice, economic democracy, cultural preservation, education,
environmental protection, and international peace. They remind the
government to act as a caretaker for its citizens, especially the
marginalized and vulnerable. For youth, understanding DPSPs is
understanding the “game rules” that shape your life opportunities,
freedoms, and societal responsibilities. They are aspirational, yes, but they
also guide real policy decisions, making them as essential to India’s
democracy as the Fundamental Rights that protect individuals.
Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy together form
the backbone of India’s Constitution. While Fundamental Rights protect
individual freedoms—speech, equality, religion, and dignity—DPSPs guide
the state to create conditions where these rights are meaningful, promoting
social justice, economic equity, education, and environmental
sustainability. Rights empower citizens; directives steer governance. For
young people, understanding both is key: rights let you claim your space,
while DPSPs show how society can be fair, inclusive, and sustainable.
Together, they ensure that liberty, equality, and fraternity aren’t just ideals
on paper, but lived realities shaping a vibrant, democratic India.

Lok Sabha
Hello everyone and welcome back.
Let me start today’s discussion with a question for you.
The moment you hear the word “Parliament,” what comes to your mind? Long speeches? Heated
arguments on television? MPs rushing into the Well of the House? Or perhaps a distant building
in New Delhi that feels far removed from your everyday life?
Now let me ask you something more personal. The price of fuel, your college fees, the
availability of jobs, internet regulations, reservation policies, or even whether your vote
matters—do you think these things are decided randomly? They are not. They are debated,
negotiated, approved, and sometimes fiercely contested in one place above all others: the Lok
Sabha. If Indian democracy had a main stage, this would be it.
Let us start with a simple idea. Imagine India as a massive group project. A really massive one.
Over 140 crore people, different languages, cultures, opinions, and expectations, all trying to live
together under one system. Now, every group project needs a place where discussions happen,
decisions are made, and responsibility is fixed when things go wrong. In India’s democracy, that
place is the Lok Sabha. Think of it as the central meeting room of the country—except the stakes
are higher than grades, and the consequences last for years.
The Lok Sabha is called the “House of the People,” and this is not just a poetic phrase. It is one
of the few institutions in India that directly represents you, whether you realise it or not. Every
time there is a general election, millions of people step out of their homes, stand in long queues,
argue over whom to vote for, and finally press a button on an electronic voting machine. That
small action sends representatives to the Lok Sabha. Those representatives speak, argue,
legislate, and sometimes misbehave, all in your name. So whether you voted enthusiastically,
reluctantly, or not at all, the Lok Sabha still shapes your reality.
India as we have established by now, follows a parliamentary system of democracy, which means
that the government is not separate from Parliament but is born out of it. Parliament itself has
two Houses: the Lok Sabha and the Rajya Sabha. You can think of them as two chambers in the
same building, but with very different personalities. The Rajya Sabha is like the experienced
senior in college—calm, reflective, and permanent. The Lok Sabha, on the other hand, is like the
elected student council—energetic, loud, constantly changing, and directly answerable to the
people who elected it. If public opinion shifts, the Lok Sabha must shift with it, or risk being
voted out.
The special status of the Lok Sabha comes from one crucial fact: its members are directly elected
by the people. This direct election gives the Lok Sabha a democratic legitimacy that no other
institution can claim in quite the same way. Judges are appointed, bureaucrats are recruited, and
even Rajya Sabha members are elected indirectly. But Lok Sabha MPs come straight from the
ballot box. They cannot forget this, at least not without consequences. Every five years, the
people have the power to say, “Thank you for your service,” or “We think someone else should
try now.”
The Constitution of India provides the legal backbone for the Lok Sabha. It lays down how the
House is composed, how long it lasts, and how it functions. According to the Constitution, the
Lok Sabha can have up to 552 members, though at present it has 543 elected members. These
members represent constituencies spread across the length and breadth of the country, from
remote border regions to densely populated urban centres. Some seats are reserved for Scheduled
Castes and Scheduled Tribes to ensure that historically marginalised communities are not
excluded from national decision-making. This reservation is not charity; it is recognition of
structural inequality and an attempt to correct it within the democratic framework.
One of the most remarkable things about the Lok Sabha is how it is formed through elections.
India is divided into parliamentary constituencies, each of which elects one Member of
Parliament. The rule governing these elections is universal adult franchise, which means that
every citizen above the age of eighteen has the right to vote. No exams, no income criteria, no
literacy test. Just citizenship and age. This decision, taken at the time of independence, was bold
and even risky by global standards. Many leaders believed that such a large, poor, and diverse
population was not “ready” for democracy. India ignored that advice, trusted its people, and built
the Lok Sabha on that trust.
General elections are conducted by the Election Commission of India, an independent
constitutional authority. Think of the Election Commission as the referee in the democratic
match. It ensures that the rules are followed, the field is level, and no one wins by cheating—at
least in theory. Once elections are completed and results are declared, the Lok Sabha comes into
existence for a term of five years. Unless, of course, it is dissolved earlier. Unlike the Rajya
Sabha, which is a permanent House and never dissolves, the Lok Sabha can be sent packing
before its full term. This feature is not a flaw; it is a democratic safeguard. It ensures that the
House never drifts too far away from the will of the people.
Inside the Lok Sabha, order is maintained by the Speaker, a figure who deserves more attention
than they usually get. The Speaker is elected by the members of the Lok Sabha and is expected
to rise above party politics. This is easier said than done, but it remains the constitutional
expectation. The Speaker decides who gets to speak, how long debates last, and how the rules of
procedure are interpreted. If the Lok Sabha were a football match, the Speaker would be the
referee, the rulebook, and sometimes the peacekeeper all rolled into one.
The Speaker also has significant constitutional powers. One of the most important is the
authority to certify whether a Bill is a Money Bill. This decision affects how the Bill moves
through Parliament and how much say the Rajya Sabha gets in the process. The Speaker’s
rulings are usually final, which makes this position extremely powerful. When the Speaker
functions impartially, the Lok Sabha becomes a space for serious democratic debate. When
neutrality is questioned, the credibility of the House itself suffers.
Now let us talk about what the Lok Sabha actually does, beyond what you see on news debates.
Its most visible function is law-making. Bills are introduced, discussed, amended, and passed in
the Lok Sabha. These laws shape almost every aspect of your life, from how universities are
regulated to how digital platforms are governed. The process is supposed to involve debate,
disagreement, and compromise. While reality does not always match the ideal, the Lok Sabha
remains the primary arena where laws are made.
Financial power is where the Lok Sabha truly stands out. In democratic systems, control over
money is control over policy. The Union Budget is presented in the Lok Sabha, and no tax can be
imposed without its approval. Money Bills can only be introduced in the Lok Sabha, not the
Rajya Sabha. This reflects a long-standing democratic principle: those who pay taxes must have
a say in how that money is spent. When you complain about fuel prices or student loans,
remember that these issues are tied directly to decisions taken in the Lok Sabha.
The Lok Sabha also keeps the government on its toes through mechanisms of accountability. The
Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers are collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. This
means they must answer questions, justify decisions, and face criticism on the floor of the House.
Question Hour, Zero Hour, debates, and motions are not just rituals; they are tools through which
power is questioned. The most dramatic of these tools is the No-Confidence Motion. If the Lok
Sabha passes such a motion, the government must resign. Few reminders of democratic power
are more direct than this.
Beyond laws and budgets, the Lok Sabha serves as a national stage for public issues. MPs raise
concerns about unemployment, inflation, farmers’ distress, women’s safety, digital privacy,
climate change, and regional inequalities. When an issue is raised in the Lok Sabha, it gains
visibility, legitimacy, and urgency. Media coverage follows, public debate intensifies, and
sometimes policy change becomes unavoidable. In this way, the Lok Sabha acts as a bridge
between society and the state.
Now let us talk about you again, because without you, none of this makes sense. Why should the
Lok Sabha matter to young people? Because decisions taken here shape the future you are
walking into. Education reforms, employment policies, startup regulations, digital governance,
and even the rules governing social media platforms are debated in this House. If young people
disengage, the Lok Sabha does not stop functioning. It simply stops reflecting youthful concerns.
It is easy to feel frustrated when you watch parliamentary proceedings. The shouting, the
walkouts, the interruptions—it can all feel like a bad reality show. But democracy is not a movie
with perfect scripts and heroic characters. It is more like a live performance, messy and
unpredictable, shaped by human behaviour. The answer to democratic imperfection is not
withdrawal, but participation. Criticism improves democracy; cynicism weakens it.
Understanding the Lok Sabha is therefore not just about exams or general knowledge. It is about
understanding your role in a system that depends on informed citizens to survive. The
Constitution gives you rights, but it also assumes responsibility. Democracy does not run on
autopilot. It requires attention, engagement, and sometimes uncomfortable conversations.
In short,
If democracy is about people’s power, the Lok Sabha is where that power is exercised.
As we come towards the end of this session, remember this: the Lok Sabha is not just a chamber
of politicians. It is a living institution shaped by history, debate, disagreement, and public
participation. It is where ideas clash, power is questioned, and the future of the country is
negotiated. When you understand the Lok Sabha, you are not just learning about Parliament. You
are learning about yourself as a citizen of India.
In the next lecture, we will turn our attention to the Parliamentary committees and procedures .
Until then, stay curious, stay informed, and remember—democracy works best when its
youngest citizens care enough to participate.

Parliamentary Committees and Procedures
When most young Indians think about Parliament, what usually comes to mind are noisy debates,
viral shouting matches, MPs holding placards, sudden walkouts, and dramatic headlines scrolling
across news channels. Parliament often looks less like a place of lawmaking and more like a
reality show where everyone is talking at the same time. But here’s the thing: what you see on
television is only the surface. The real work of Indian democracy happens quietly, patiently, and
often away from the cameras, inside something called parliamentary committees. If Parliament
were a massive startup run by 543 founders, then parliamentary committees would be the core
teams doing the brainstorming, debugging, and quality checks before any big launch.
India is a country of 1.4 billion people, with complex social problems, economic challenges,
regional diversity, and competing interests. Expecting the full Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha to
examine every bill, budget line, and policy detail in depth is like expecting a full classroom of
students to write one assignment together on a single laptop. It’s simply not practical.
Parliamentary committees exist because democracy, like life, needs smaller groups to function
better. They allow MPs to sit down, slow things down, ask uncomfortable questions, consult
experts, and actually understand what a law will do before it is passed.
At its core, a parliamentary committee is a group of Members of Parliament tasked with
examining specific subjects, ministries, bills, or issues. These committees cut across party lines,
which means members from the ruling party and the opposition sit together. Interestingly, when
MPs enter committee rooms, party slogans usually take a back seat. The atmosphere is less about
political scoring and more about problem-solving. Think of it like a group project where even
rivals have to work together because the grade depends on it.
Parliamentary committees in India broadly fall into two categories: standing committees and ad
hoc committees. Standing committees are permanent in nature. They function year after year and
are reconstituted periodically. Ad hoc committees, on the other hand, are temporary. They are
created for a specific purpose and dissolved once their task is complete. A simple analogy would
be permanent staff versus project-based consultants. Both are important, but they serve different
purposes.
Among standing committees, the most influential are the Departmentally Related Standing
Committees. There are 24 such committees, each linked to specific ministries like Finance,
Home Affairs, Defence, Education, Health, Agriculture, and Information Technology. These
committees examine bills related to their ministries, scrutinize budget allocations, assess policy
implementation, and submit reports to Parliament. For young citizens, this is important because
many policies that directly affect students, job seekers, entrepreneurs, and digital users are
shaped inside these committees long before they become law.
Take education policy as an example. When the National Education Policy was being discussed,
the Standing Committee on Education examined issues related to higher education funding,
university autonomy, digital learning infrastructure, and access for marginalized communities.
The committee heard inputs from education experts, administrators, and stakeholders. While not
all recommendations are accepted by the government, the process ensures that policy does not
emerge out of thin air. It is debated, questioned, and revised.
One of the most talked-about recent examples is the examination of India’s data protection
framework. The Personal Data Protection Bill was referred to a Joint Parliamentary Committee,
which included members from both Houses. This committee took inputs from technology
companies, civil society groups, privacy experts, and government officials. The discussions
ranged from data sovereignty and surveillance to user consent and corporate accountability. For
young Indians who live their lives online, this committee’s work had real consequences for
privacy, social media, and digital rights.
Joint Parliamentary Committees, or JPCs, are a type of ad hoc committee. They are formed when
an issue is considered too important, controversial, or complex to be handled routinely. A famous
historical example is the Joint Parliamentary Committee on the Bofors scandal in the late 1980s,
which investigated allegations of corruption in defence procurement. More recently, JPCs have
been constituted to examine matters such as financial irregularities, telecom licensing issues, and
data protection laws. When Parliament sets up a JPC, it signals that the issue requires deeper
investigation beyond regular debate.
Another critical category of standing committees relates to financial oversight. These include the
Public Accounts Committee, the Estimates Committee, and the Committee on Public
Undertakings. These committees act like auditors of the government. The Public Accounts
Committee, for instance, examines how public money is spent and whether it is used for the
purpose approved by Parliament. It relies heavily on reports from the Comptroller and Auditor
General of India. If democracy had a watchdog that doesn’t bark loudly but bites effectively, this
committee would be it.
For example, when audit reports reveal irregularities in government spending on infrastructure
projects or welfare schemes, the Public Accounts Committee calls officials, questions them in
detail, and demands explanations. These proceedings are not glamorous, but they are crucial.
They ensure accountability and discourage misuse of public funds. For young taxpayers entering
the workforce, this oversight matters more than most people realize.
Parliamentary committees also play a vital role in examining bills clause by clause. When a bill
is introduced in Parliament, it may be referred to a standing committee for detailed scrutiny. The
committee examines each provision, identifies ambiguities, and suggests amendments. This
process is especially important for technically complex laws such as environmental regulations,
digital governance frameworks, or financial reforms. Without committee scrutiny, laws risk
being vague, poorly drafted, or disconnected from ground realities.
A strong example is environmental legislation. Standing committees examining
environment-related bills have raised concerns about environmental clearances, tribal rights, and
long-term ecological impact. These committees often invite environmental scientists, activists,
and industry representatives to present their views. This creates a space where multiple
perspectives can be considered, something that is difficult to achieve in a noisy parliamentary
debate.
Now let’s talk about parliamentary procedures, because committees do not function randomly.
They operate within a structured framework governed by rules, conventions, and constitutional
provisions. Parliamentary procedure refers to the formal processes through which laws are
introduced, debated, examined, and passed. It includes everything from question hour and zero
hour to motions, voting procedures, and committee referrals.
For youth, one of the most interesting procedural tools is Question Hour. This is the time when
MPs ask questions to ministers about government functioning. While Question Hour happens on
the floor of the House, many of these questions feed into committee discussions later. When MPs
consistently raise issues related to unemployment data, education funding, or digital
infrastructure, committees often take these concerns up for deeper examination.
Another important procedural aspect is the referral of bills to committees. While not mandatory,
this practice has traditionally strengthened legislative quality. Committees provide a non-partisan
space where MPs can agree on technical improvements even if they disagree politically. In recent
years, there has been debate over declining referrals of bills to committees, raising concerns
among scholars and former parliamentarians about reduced scrutiny.
A relatable analogy here is exams without internal assessments. If final exams are the only
evaluation, students may pass, but learning suffers. Committees act like internal assessments for
laws, improving quality before the final vote. When this step is skipped, the system still
functions, but with weaker foundations.
Committees also serve as training grounds for MPs, especially first-time members. A young MP
sitting on a committee learns how policies are framed, how bureaucrats function, and how laws
impact different sectors. This behind-the-scenes learning rarely makes headlines, but it
strengthens democratic capacity over time.
From a youth perspective, parliamentary committees matter because they connect governance
with expertise. Committees frequently consult external experts, researchers, and stakeholders.
This means that academics, professionals, and even civil society groups can influence policy
without contesting elections. It is one of the few spaces where knowledge can directly shape
power.
Despite their importance, committees face challenges. Limited time, increasing workloads,
declining referrals, and political polarization sometimes weaken their effectiveness. Reports are
occasionally ignored, and recommendations are not always implemented. However, even when
their influence seems limited, committees create institutional memory and documented critique
that shapes future debates.
For young citizens, understanding parliamentary committees changes how you view democracy.
It shifts focus from personalities to processes, from drama to design. It reminds us that
democracy is not just about voting every five years but about continuous scrutiny, discussion,
and accountability.
So the next time you see a heated parliamentary debate trending online, remember that
somewhere behind that noise, a committee is quietly reading reports, questioning officials, and
trying to make sense of complex realities. Parliamentary committees may not trend on social
media, but they are the reason Indian democracy continues to function despite its chaos.
Understanding committees and procedures is not just for civil service aspirants or law students. It
is for anyone who wants to be an informed citizen. Because democracy does not survive on
passion alone. It survives on patience, process, and participation. And parliamentary committees
are where all three quietly come together.

Articles 52 to 61 of the Indian Constitution collectively lay down the framework for the
office of the President of India — the highest constitutional authority and the head of the
Union Executive. Article 52 establishes the position of the President, while Article 53
vests in them the executive power of the Union. Articles 54 and 55 describe the process
and manner of the President’s election, ensuring representation from both Parliament and
the States. Article 56 defines the term of office, and Article 57 provides for eligibility for
re-election. The qualifications required to hold this esteemed office are detailed in Article
58, while Article 59 specifies the conditions under which the President shall serve.
Before assuming office, the President takes an oath or affirmation under Article 60 to
preserve, protect, and defend the Constitution and the law. Finally, Article 61 outlines the
solemn and detailed procedure for the impeachment of the President, ensuring that the
dignity of the office is upheld through constitutional accountability.
Let us know look at the various Roles, powers and functions of the President
Role of the President
The President is the head of the State, the supreme commander of the armed forces,
and above all, the guardian of the Constitution.
Though the President does not rule directly, every act of governance — every law, every
appointment, every ordinance — flows through their name.
As Dr. Ambedkar explained,
“The President occupies the same position as the King under the British
Constitution.”
That means the President acts on the advice of the Council of Ministers, led by the
Prime Minister — yet remains the moral compass of the system.
Let us look at the powers and functions of the President
Executive Powers:
The President appoints the Prime Minister, the Council of Ministers, Governors of
States, Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts, and Ambassadors.
All executive actions of the Government of India are taken in the President’s name.
Legislative Powers:
The President summons and dissolves Parliament, addresses both Houses, and gives
assent to bills.
And when Parliament is not in session — the President can issue Ordinances under
Article 123, carrying the force of law.
Financial Powers:
No Money Bill can be introduced without the President’s recommendation.
The Union Budget is presented in the President’s name, and the President appoints the
Finance Commission every five years.
Judicial Powers:
The President has the power to grant pardons, reprieves, or remissions of punishment
— especially in death penalty cases.
This reflects the humane side of the Indian Constitution.
Diplomatic & Military Powers:
As the supreme commander, the President can declare war or peace, and represents
India in all international affairs — receiving foreign envoys and signing treaties.
Emergency Powers:
And in extraordinary times, the Constitution gives the President extraordinary powers —
● Under Article 352, a National Emergency for war or rebellion.
● Under Article 356, President’s Rule in states.
● And under Article 360, a Financial Emergency to protect economic stability.
Practical Role
In normal times, the President acts as a symbol of unity and constitutional balance.
But in crises — like a hung Parliament or national emergency — the President’s
discretion becomes decisive.
Dr. S. Radhakrishnan captured it best when he said:
“The President represents the national purpose — the permanent and unifying
element of the State.”
So, the President may not govern day-to-day, but stands as the moral and constitutional
guardian of India’s democracy.

Dear learners, welcome to another week with another
interesting topic - The Prime Minister of India — The
Power at the Centre
If the President is the heart of India’s Constitution, then
the Prime Minister is its mind — dynamic, decisive, and
political. In the world’s largest democracy, the real power
does not lie in the crown or the ceremonial office, but in
the mandate of the people. The Prime Minister stands at
the very centre of this democratic mandate, steering the
course of governance and embodying the political will of
the nation.
Our first Prime Minister, Pandit Jawaharlal Nehru, once
said,
“The future beckons to us. Whither do we go and
what shall be our endeavour?” That question continues
to define the spirit of the office. The Prime Minister of
India is not merely an administrator; he or she is the
navigator who determines the direction in which the
nation must move. Nehru’s vision of modern India laid the
foundations of a parliamentary democracy rooted in
progress, science, and freedom — a legacy that
continues to guide those who have held this position
since.
In India’s parliamentary system, the Prime Minister is the
real executive authority — the head of the Council of
Ministers, the chief policy-maker, and the leader who
bridges the will of the people with the functioning of the
state. The office connects the President and the
Parliament, ensuring coherence in governance and
coordination among ministries. As the constitutional
expert M. V. Pylee aptly remarked,
“The Prime Minister is
the keystone of the Cabinet arch. Remove him, and the
whole structure collapses.”
Articles 74 to 78 of the Indian Constitution define the structure,
powers, and responsibilities of the Prime Minister and the Council of
Ministers, who together form the real executive authority of the Union.
Article 74(1) provides that there shall be a Council of Ministers with
the Prime Minister at its head to aid and advise the President, while
Article 74(2) ensures the confidentiality of such advice by prohibiting
any court from inquiring into it. Under Article 75(1), the Prime Minister
is appointed by the President, and other ministers are appointed on
the Prime Minister’s advice, signifying the central role of the office.
The Prime Minister occupies a unique constitutional
position, where power and responsibility converge. As
the head of the Council of Ministers under Article 74 of
the Constitution, the Prime Minister advises the President
in the exercise of executive powers. It is the Prime
Minister who recommends the appointment of ministers,
allocates their portfolios, and can request their
resignation. This authority ensures that the Cabinet
functions as a unified whole, guided by a single vision
and leadership. The Prime Minister chairs Cabinet
meetings, settles disputes among ministers, and
provides policy direction across sectors — from defence
and finance to education and foreign affairs.
In the legislative sphere, the Prime Minister is the leader
of the Lok Sabha and determines the legislative agenda
of the government. They decide which bills are to be
introduced, when Parliament will be summoned or
prorogued, and may advise the President to dissolve the
Lok Sabha, paving the way for fresh elections. The Prime
Minister also plays a pivotal role in debates, policy
statements, and question hours, serving as the chief
spokesperson of the government in Parliament.
Financially, the Prime Minister’s role is central and
far-reaching. Every budget, every Five-Year Plan, every
major economic reform carries their imprint. From
Nehru’s vision of a planned economy to Indira Gandhi’s
nationalization of banks, from Manmohan Singh’s
liberalization to Narendra Modi’s Digital India and Make in
India initiatives — each era of economic governance has
borne the distinct signature of the Prime Minister’s
economic philosophy.
In the field of foreign affairs, the Prime Minister
represents India on the global stage — negotiating
treaties, attending international summits, and shaping
strategic partnerships. The foreign policy of India has
often reflected the personality and vision of its Prime
Ministers: Nehru’s Non-Alignment, Indira Gandhi’s
assertive diplomacy, Vajpayee’s peace initiatives with
neighbours, and Modi’s emphasis on connectivity,
diaspora engagement, and India’s global leadership. As
Vajpayee wisely said,
“You can change friends, but not
neighbours,
” reminding us that diplomacy requires both
principle and pragmatism.
Politically, the Prime Minister serves as the leader of the
ruling party or coalition. It is through this position that
they ensure unity within the party, maintain coordination
among allies, and communicate the government’s
policies to the people. The Prime Minister is the bridge
between the government and the citizens — the voice
that translates complex policies into a vision people can
believe in.
In coalition governments, the Prime Minister acts as a
negotiator and consensus-builder, balancing multiple
parties and ideologies to sustain stability. In majority
governments, the office becomes the defining voice of
governance, setting the national agenda and direction.
Each Prime Minister — from Nehru’s visionary
modernism, Indira Gandhi’s centralization of authority,
and Vajpayee’s statesmanship, to Modi’s emphasis on
strong leadership and direct public engagement — has
redefined the contours of this powerful office in their own
way.
The Prime Minister’s role extends beyond policy and
politics. They are the moral and symbolic leader of the
nation — the first to console the country in moments of
tragedy and the first to inspire it in moments of triumph.
Every word spoken, every decision taken, reverberates
across the institutions of governance and into the daily
lives of citizens.
The office of the Prime Minister is, therefore, not merely
administrative but transformative. It reflects the nation’s
aspirations, challenges, and evolving identity. Leadership
here is not just about power; it is about vision,
responsibility, and wisdom. The Prime Minister embodies
the spirit of India in motion, while the President
symbolizes its constitutional soul. Together, they sustain
the rhythm of the Republic — steady, strong, and free.
Roles and Functions of the Prime Minister of India
1. Executive Role
● The Prime Minister is the head of the Council of
Ministers and exercises real executive authority in
the government.
● Appoints ministers, allocates and reshuffles their
portfolios.
● Advises the President on all executive matters and
on important appointments such as Governors,
Ambassadors, and other key officials.
● Chairs Cabinet meetings, coordinates its work, and
ensures unity of purpose and direction among
ministries.
● Acts as a link between the President and the Council
of Ministers, communicating decisions and advice.
2. Legislative Role
● Acts as the leader of the Lok Sabha (House of the
People).
● Determines the legislative agenda of the government
and introduces important bills and policies in
Parliament.
● Advises the President on summoning, proroguing, or
dissolving the Lok Sabha.
● Plays a major role in policy formulation,
parliamentary debates, and law-making.
● Represents the government during question hours
and motions of confidence in Parliament.
3. Financial Role
● Plays a decisive role in the preparation and
presentation of the Union Budget.
● Guides the country’s economic and development
policies, such as Five-Year Plans (earlier), reforms,
and welfare schemes.
● Oversees financial management, economic strategy,
and resource allocation across ministries.
4. Foreign Policy Role
● Represents India in international affairs, global
summits, and diplomatic negotiations.
● Plays a key role in formulating and implementing
India’s foreign policy and external relations.
● Engages with world leaders to promote India’s
strategic, economic, and security interests abroad.
5. Political Role
● Serves as the leader of the ruling party or coalition,
ensuring party unity and discipline.
● Acts as the chief spokesperson and political
communicator of the government.
● Leads the party in elections, campaigns, and public
outreach efforts.
● Balances relationships between the party,
Parliament, and the people.

Cabinet
Let’s be honest — when most people hear the word “Cabinet,” they think of wooden cupboards
or those furniture ads with a “Diwali discount.” But in politics, the Cabinet isn’t something that
holds plates — it’s something that holds power. Real, tangible, decision-making power that
literally shapes the direction of India as a nation.
So, let’s unlock that metaphorical cabinet and see what’s inside — the Cabinet of India, the nerve
centre of governance, the powerhouse behind government decisions, and arguably, one of the
most fascinating parts of our parliamentary democracy.
To understand what the Cabinet is today, we need to go back a bit in history — to the British era,
when the seeds of this system were planted. During the colonial period, the British introduced a
system of governance in India that mirrored their own. The Governor-General’s Executive
Council, formed in 1773, was the first version of what we now call the Cabinet. Of course, it
wasn’t democratic — the members weren’t elected and mostly served as advisors to the
Governor-General, who acted on behalf of the British Crown. Still, it established a pattern — a
small body of key officials assisting the head of the government in decision-making.
Fast forward to 1947, the year of India’s independence. The newly formed nation needed a
political framework that balanced leadership, accountability, and efficiency. The Constituent
Assembly, while drafting the Constitution, decided to adapt the Westminster model of
parliamentary democracy — the same model used in Britain, where the executive is drawn from
the legislature, and the Cabinet becomes the core decision-making body. That’s how India’s
Cabinet system was born — not as a colonial leftover, but as a reimagined tool for democratic
governance.
Now, if the President of India is the ceremonial head, the Prime Minister is the captain of the
ship, and the Cabinet? Well, they’re the ones who actually steer the ship, set the course, and
decide how fast or slow it moves. Under Article 74(1) of the Indian Constitution, there “shall be
a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at the head to aid and advise the President.” But
among that Council, a smaller, elite group called the Cabinet wields the real power. Think of it as
the “inner circle” — like the main cast in a long-running show where side characters exist, but
the core team drives the plot.
So, who’s in this “main cast”? The Cabinet includes the Prime Minister and the Cabinet
Ministers, each heading important ministries like Home Affairs, Finance, Defence, External
Affairs, and so on. These are the heavyweights of governance. While the Council of Ministers
may include Ministers of State and Deputy Ministers, they mostly handle specific segments or
assist the Cabinet. The Cabinet itself makes the key policy decisions, sets the national agenda,
and coordinates the functioning of all ministries.
The Prime Minister stands at the centre of this structure. Constitutionally, the Cabinet is a
collective body, but politically, it revolves around the Prime Minister. Every Cabinet Minister
holds office “during the pleasure of the President,” but in practice, it’s really at the discretion of
the PM. This makes the Cabinet both collective in responsibility and centralised in leadership.
It’s like a band where everyone plays an instrument, but the Prime Minister is the lead vocalist
and composer — if they change the tune, the others must follow.
This idea of collective responsibility is central to the Cabinet system. As per Article 75(3), the
Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. This means if one Cabinet
decision fails or is rejected by the lower house, the entire Cabinet must resign. There’s no
passing the blame — they stand and fall together. It’s the political equivalent of “one for all, all
for one.” This system ensures that the executive remains accountable to the legislature — a
democratic safeguard that prevents arbitrary power.
But what exactly does the Cabinet do? The short answer: everything that matters. The Cabinet is
the highest decision-making authority in India’s executive structure. It decides on policies,
approves bills before they’re introduced in Parliament, manages crises, oversees national
security, and makes key appointments. Every major government initiative — from
demonetisation to digitalisation, foreign policy to welfare schemes — first passes through the
Cabinet’s lens.
Cabinet meetings, though confidential, are where the real debates happen. Ministers present
proposals from their ministries, and the Prime Minister moderates discussions to reach a
consensus. Once a decision is made, it becomes binding on all members of the Cabinet, whether
they agree with it or not. This is again where the principle of collective responsibility comes into
play — internal disagreements stay inside the room. In public, the Cabinet must present a united
front.
Now, while the Constitution doesn’t explicitly mention the term “Cabinet” in detail, the 44th
Amendment Act of 1978 formally recognised it. The amendment inserted Article 352(3), which
requires that a written recommendation for declaring a National Emergency must come
specifically from the Cabinet — not just any minister. This distinction reinforced that only the
top decision-making body — the Cabinet — could make such serious recommendations,
ensuring checks and balances within the executive itself.
Over the decades, the Cabinet’s role has evolved with changing political realities. During
Jawaharlal Nehru’s era, the Cabinet worked like a team of equals. Nehru valued debate,
encouraged independent opinions, and often allowed differences to surface openly before
consensus was reached. Under Indira Gandhi, however, the system became more centralised —
the Cabinet was often overshadowed by her personal authority, and decision-making became
highly individualised. This trend has ebbed and flowed over time, depending on the strength of
the Prime Minister’s leadership and the nature of coalition or majority governments.
In coalition eras like the 1990s, Cabinets often reflected delicate power-sharing arrangements.
The Prime Minister had to balance regional parties and multiple ideologies. This sometimes
diluted the Cabinet’s collective unity, turning it into more of a negotiation platform than a
decision-making powerhouse. But post-2014, with a strong majority government, the Cabinet has
again become more centralised — streamlined for efficiency, though occasionally criticised for
limited internal dissent.
Beyond politics, the Cabinet plays an administrative role that’s often underappreciated. It
coordinates across ministries, ensuring that policies don’t overlap or contradict each other. For
instance, if the Ministry of Environment introduces a policy affecting industrial expansion, the
Cabinet ensures it aligns with the Ministry of Commerce and Industry’s growth plans. It also
handles inter-state issues, international treaties, and appointments to key constitutional bodies.
Essentially, the Cabinet is the final checkpoint before a policy becomes law or practice.
There’s also the fascinating ritual of Cabinet Committees — smaller, specialised groups formed
to handle specific areas like security, economy, or parliamentary affairs. These committees allow
for focused discussions without involving the entire Cabinet in every detail. Some are
permanent, like the Cabinet Committee on Security (CCS), which handles defence and national
security, while others are temporary, formed for particular issues. These committees reflect the
Cabinet’s adaptability — its ability to manage both broad strategy and detailed policy work.
In comparing India’s Cabinet to that of other democracies, one finds interesting contrasts. In the
United States, the Cabinet is more like an advisory body to the President, with members
(Secretaries) not drawn from the legislature. In the United Kingdom, the Cabinet functions
similarly to India’s, as both follow the Westminster model. But India’s diversity, scale, and
federal structure make its Cabinet far more complex — balancing regional aspirations, coalition
pressures, and national priorities simultaneously. It’s like managing a group project where every
member represents a different state, ideology, and sometimes even language — and yet, the
outcome has to be coherent.
Now, let’s not romanticise it too much — the Cabinet system isn’t perfect. Critics argue that the
increasing dominance of the Prime Minister’s Office (PMO) has reduced the Cabinet’s
autonomy. Many major decisions today are seen as being driven by the PM and a close circle of
advisors rather than the Cabinet collectively. This “presidentialisation” of the parliamentary
system, as some scholars call it, raises questions about the balance of power. But at the same
time, supporters argue that strong leadership makes governance more efficient in a country as
vast and complex as India.
The Cabinet also mirrors India’s political culture. It’s a microcosm of representation —
balancing caste, religion, gender, and regional diversity. Having ministers from different states
and social backgrounds is not just symbolic; it’s strategic, ensuring that policies resonate across
India’s multifaceted society. The inclusion of women and younger leaders in recent Cabinets
signals slow but steady progress toward inclusivity.
And yet, despite all its internal dynamics, secrecy surrounds the Cabinet’s workings. The Official
Secrets Act protects the confidentiality of Cabinet discussions, ensuring that debates within
remain private until policies are announced. This confidentiality helps ministers speak freely
without political backlash but sometimes fuels public criticism about transparency.
When we look at the Cabinet in action — whether during economic reforms, crises like
COVID-19, or foreign policy decisions — it becomes clear that this institution is not just a
bureaucratic mechanism; it’s the beating heart of Indian democracy. Every Cabinet decision
trickles down into everyday life — from how taxes are structured to how education policies
evolve or how national security is handled. It’s the reason petrol prices, digital policies, and even
the content you see on social media can, in some way, be traced back to a Cabinet decision.
In popular culture, the Cabinet rarely gets cinematic attention. We see films on elections,
freedom struggles, and political scandals, but not on the quiet, powerful rooms where the
country’s direction is decided. Maybe because Cabinet politics is less about spectacle and more
about substance. It’s where politics turns into policy — the point where promises made to the
people start transforming into programmes and actions.
To wrap it all up, the Cabinet of India is both the symbol and substance of responsible
government. It’s where democracy meets decision-making, where power meets accountability.
From its colonial roots to its contemporary form, the Cabinet has evolved as the central pillar of
India’s governance — adapting, reforming, and surviving the tests of time and politics. Whether
under a coalition’s cautious balance or a majority government’s strong command, the Cabinet
remains the defining force that converts political vision into administrative reality.
So the next time you hear “Cabinet meeting” in the news, know that it’s not just another
bureaucratic ritual — it’s where India’s future is being scripted, debated, and decided. Behind
those closed doors, policies that shape millions of lives are born. It’s the place where the idea of
India, as a living, breathing democracy, finds its daily rhythm.
And that’s what makes the Cabinet not just a political institution, but the real engine room of the
world’s largest democracy.

Vice- President
When you hear the phrase “Vice President of India,” what comes to mind? For most people, it’s a
polite shrug or a hesitant, “Oh, they’re second to the President, right?” And while that isn’t
wrong, it’s only scratching the surface. The Vice President of India is not just a stand-in for the
President; they play a crucial, often underappreciated role in keeping the country’s democratic
machinery running smoothly. Today, we’re decoding this fascinating office — who the Vice
President is, what they do, how they got there, and why their position matters more than you
might think.
Let’s start with the basics. The Vice President of India holds the second-highest constitutional
office in the country, right after the President. But unlike the President, who is primarily a
ceremonial figurehead, the Vice President has a dual identity — one foot in the Executive and
another in the Legislature. In other words, they serve as a bridge between two branches of
government. The Vice President is also the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, which
means that they preside over the Upper House of Parliament. This dual role gives the office a
unique blend of authority and restraint.
Imagine the Parliament as a lively debate arena — sometimes intense, sometimes chaotic. The
Vice President, as the Rajya Sabha’s presiding officer, is the one who ensures that the debates do
not descend into a shouting match. They maintain order, uphold decorum, and make sure that
India’s democracy, in all its noisy diversity, stays within the rules of procedure.
But where did this office come from? To understand that, we need to rewind to the late 1940s,
when the Indian Constitution was being drafted. The Constituent Assembly, inspired by several
global models, borrowed the idea of a Vice President from the United States, where the Vice
President also serves as the President of the Senate. However, India didn’t just copy-paste the
concept. It added a touch of constitutional creativity. The drafters wanted a figure who could step
in as President when required, but who would otherwise contribute meaningfully to legislative
functioning. Thus, Articles 63 to 71 of the Indian Constitution — located in Part V, Chapter I —
define the role, powers, and responsibilities of the Vice President.
Article 63 simply declares that there shall be a Vice President of India. Article 64 assigns them
the role of ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. Article 65 outlines that they can act as
President in case of a vacancy or absence in that office. These provisions may seem simple, but
together, they form the foundation of a position that balances constitutional continuity and
parliamentary integrity.
Now, how does one become the Vice President of India? It’s not a job you can get by popularity
polls or social media clout. The process is deliberately institutional. The Vice President is elected
by an Electoral College consisting of the members of both Houses of Parliament — the Lok
Sabha and the Rajya Sabha. Unlike the election of the President, which also involves the State
Legislative Assemblies, the Vice President’s election is limited to Members of Parliament. The
voting system used is proportional representation by means of a single transferable vote. In
simple terms, MPs rank candidates in order of preference, and votes are redistributed until one
candidate secures the required quota.
To qualify for the post, an individual must be an Indian citizen, at least thirty-five years old, and
eligible to be a member of the Rajya Sabha. It’s an election that prizes credibility, parliamentary
experience, and statesmanship over popularity.
India’s first Vice President, elected in 1952, was Dr. Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan — philosopher,
scholar, statesman, and one of the intellectual giants of modern India. His tenure set a precedent
for what this office could represent: a blend of intellect, restraint, and moral authority. He later
went on to become the President of India, establishing an early pattern where the Vice
Presidency often served as a stepping-stone to the presidency.
Over the decades, the office has been occupied by many notable figures — Dr. Zakir Husain, B.
D. Jatti, Krishan Kant, Hamid Ansari, M. Venkaiah Naidu — and most recently, Jagdeep
Dhankhar, who assumed office in August 2022. Dhankhar, a lawyer by profession and former
Governor of West Bengal, is known for his assertive and outspoken style. His tenure so far has
brought renewed attention to the Rajya Sabha, not least because of his sharp exchanges and
insistence on parliamentary discipline — moments that occasionally light up social media trends.
So, what exactly does the Vice President do on a daily basis? Their primary duty, as the
Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, is to preside over its sessions. This is a responsibility that demands
both constitutional knowledge and political tact. They must ensure that the proceedings are
orderly, that members follow the rules, and that discussions remain substantive rather than
descending into chaos. In moments of uproar — and let’s be honest, Indian parliamentary
sessions can sometimes resemble reality TV — the Vice President’s calm authority is what
restores decorum.
Beyond the chairing of sessions, the Vice President decides on points of order, interprets rules of
procedure, and ensures that all political parties get fair time and representation. This might sound
bureaucratic, but it is vital to the functioning of Indian democracy. The Rajya Sabha, unlike the
Lok Sabha, is not subject to dissolution. It represents the states and serves as a check on hasty
legislation. By guiding its debates and maintaining procedural order, the Vice President
contributes directly to legislative quality and federal balance.
The second, and perhaps more dramatic, role of the Vice President emerges when the President’s
office falls vacant or the President is unable to perform their duties. In such cases, the Vice
President steps in as the Acting President of India. This is not a symbolic role — during that
period, they wield all the powers of the President, from assenting to bills to appointing ministers.
However, this arrangement is temporary, lasting only until a new President is elected. Once that
happens, the Vice President returns to their original duties in the Rajya Sabha.
This dual nature of the office — legislative on most days, executive in times of necessity —
gives the Vice Presidency its distinctive importance. It is an office that stands ready, yet
restrained; powerful, yet deliberately limited by constitutional design. Unlike in the United
States, where the Vice President is a key part of the executive branch and often a political
heavyweight in their own right, the Indian Vice President’s role is intentionally more detached
from daily governance. Their authority lies not in making policy but in maintaining institutional
order.
Yet, this doesn’t make the position insignificant. In fact, it may be one of the most quietly
influential roles in the system. Presiding over the Rajya Sabha gives the Vice President a
front-row seat to national policy debates, legislative innovations, and political trends. They
witness the evolution of laws, the clash of ideologies, and the theatre of democracy in its raw
form. Their decisions — on procedural matters, admissibility of motions, or disciplinary actions
— can shape the very tone of parliamentary democracy.
It’s also worth noting that the Rajya Sabha itself occupies a unique space in India’s federal
structure. It represents the interests of the states and serves as a reviewing chamber for legislation
passed by the Lok Sabha. The Vice President, as its Chairman, thus embodies the spirit of
cooperative federalism. In moments when political tempers run high or when the balance
between the Union and the states seems strained, the Vice President’s impartiality becomes
crucial.
Historically, some Vice Presidents have elevated the dignity of the office through intellectual
leadership. Dr. Radhakrishnan used his speeches to reflect on the moral and philosophical
foundations of democracy. Hamid Ansari, during his two terms, emphasized pluralism, tolerance,
and parliamentary civility. Venkaiah Naidu, known for his rhetorical flair and humor, often
reminded members of the need for discipline and debate over disruption. Each brought a distinct
personality to the chair, proving that the Vice Presidency is as much about character as it is about
constitutional text.
Comparing the Vice President to the President and the Prime Minister helps to clarify their
relative roles. The President is the head of state — the ceremonial embodiment of India’s unity,
who exercises powers largely on the advice of the Council of Ministers. The Prime Minister is
the head of government — the real executive authority, responsible for policymaking and
administration. The Vice President, by contrast, stands at the intersection of both realms:
constitutionally next to the President but functionally part of the Legislature. They don’t wield
executive power like the Prime Minister, nor do they symbolize the state like the President.
Instead, they ensure that the legislative process — the heartbeat of democracy — remains steady
and functional.
This makes the Vice President’s role somewhat paradoxical — immensely important but rarely
dramatic. Their power lies in discretion, not dominance. In an age of instant news and political
spectacle, that kind of quiet influence doesn’t always get attention. Yet, when viewed through a
constitutional lens, it represents one of the most elegant designs of India’s democratic system —
the idea that authority need not always shout to be significant.
The evolution of the office also mirrors India’s own political journey. From the early decades of
idealistic nation-building to the present era of coalition politics and strong regional voices, the
Vice President’s chair has witnessed the shifting tides of Indian democracy. The occupant of that
chair must balance neutrality with firmness, tradition with adaptability, and constitutional
restraint with moral courage.
In today’s politically polarized climate, this balance is more crucial than ever. The Vice
President’s ability to remain impartial — to respect opposition voices even while maintaining
order — can help reinforce public faith in democratic institutions. As Rajya Sabha Chairman,
they have the power to set the tone of political discourse, to remind lawmakers that disagreement
need not mean disrespect.
At a time when social media amplifies every confrontation and political debates often turn
performative, the Vice President’s calm enforcement of procedure becomes a quiet act of
democratic preservation. It may not trend on X, but it strengthens the constitutional spine of the
Republic.
Beyond Parliament, the Vice President also represents India at official functions, delivers
speeches on national occasions, and participates in educational and cultural events. These may
seem ceremonial, but they reflect another dimension of the office — as a symbol of dignity,
intellectual engagement, and continuity. Dr. Radhakrishnan’s philosophical reflections, Zakir
Husain’s emphasis on education, and Hamid Ansari’s advocacy for inclusivity all show how the
Vice Presidency can serve as a platform for national reflection.
If the President embodies the idea of India as a nation, the Vice President reflects the functioning
of India as a democracy. The President signs the bills, but the Vice President often witnesses the
debates that shape those bills. The President addresses the Parliament once a year, but the Vice
President listens to it almost every day. That makes their understanding of the political pulse of
the nation uniquely nuanced.
In conclusion, the Vice President of India may not always occupy the headlines, but their
influence runs deep. They are the custodian of parliamentary ethics, the sentinel of legislative
decorum, and the constitutional safety net for continuity of governance. From Radhakrishnan’s
moral intellect to Dhankhar’s assertive authority, the office has evolved with the times but
retained its core purpose — to uphold the dignity of democracy.
So, the next time someone shrugs and says, “What does the Vice President even do?”, you can
tell them this: they keep the world’s largest democracy from descending into chaos — with a
gavel, a microphone, and an unshakable commitment to balance. In a political system filled with
power players and headline makers, the Vice President remains India’s quiet constitutional
guardian — the unsung stabilizer in the noisy symphony of democracy.

a) Understanding State Governance (PM)
Meaning of State Governance in India
In the Indian constitutional setup, state governance refers to how States
(not the Union) are governed under the Constitution of India. India follows a
federal system with a strong Centre, where powers are divided between the
Union and the States.
Constitutional Basis
● Articles 152–237 of the Constitution deal with State Governments
● States are not sovereign; they derive authority from the Constitution
● Governance is carried out in the name of the Governor, but real
power lies with the elected government
Structure of State Government
1. Governor (Articles 153–162) - He/She is appointed by the President of
India; is the Constitutional (nominal) head of the State; and Acts on the aid
and advice of the Council of Ministers
Key powers of the Governor Are - To appoint Chief Minister; Summon and
dissolve State Legislature; Gives assent to State bills. The Governor can
reserve certain bills for the President and has discretionary powers in
specific situations (e.g., hung assembly). The Governor is often described
as the link between the Union and the State.
2. Council of Ministers (Articles 163–164) It is headed by the Chief
Minister and is the Real executive authority of the State - Collectively
responsible to the State Legislative Assembly
1
Functions of the Council of Ministers is to formulate and implement
state policies; run administration; prepare state budget and advise the
Governor.
3. State Legislature (Articles 168–212)
There can be two types of State Legislatures
1. Unicameral – Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha)
2. Bicameral – Legislative Assembly + Legislative Council (Vidhan
Parishad)
Legislative Assembly is - Directly elected by people; Controls Council of
Ministers; and is the place where Money bills originate.
Legislative Council (where present) - is a Permanent body and has
limited powers compared to Assembly.
4. State Judiciary (Articles 214–231) This is headed by the High Court -
is Independent from executive and legislature. The State Judiciary ensures
- Rule of law; Protection of Fundamental Rights and Judicial review of state
actions.
Distribution of Powers in the Indian Constitution lies in the Seventh
Schedule which divides subjects into:
1. Union List – Defence, foreign affairs, currency
2. State List – Police, public order, health, agriculture
3. Concurrent List – Education, forests, criminal law
In case of conflict on Concurrent List, Union law prevails.
Federal Features Affecting State Governance
2
Strong Centre Bias - Seen in Article 356 – President’s Rule; All-India
Services (IAS, IPS); Emergency provisions; and the fact that Governor
appointed by Centre. Because of this, India is often called a
“quasi-federal” system.
Role of Panchayats and Municipalities is laid down in the 73rd & 74th
Constitutional Amendments. They introduced local self-governance
and laid down that the States are responsible for - Organising local
bodies and Devolving powers and funds.
Challenges in State Governance can be many like - Centre–State
conflicts; Role of Governor controversies; Fiscal dependency on Centre;
Law and order pressures; and implementation gaps in welfare schemes
But finally we can say that the Significance of State Governance is
that states directly affect citizens’ daily lives. As the State plays a key role
in - Development - Law and order - Social welfare. Hence effective state
governance strengthens Indian federalism.

a) Local Governance & Panchayati Raj Institutions
Let us begin by first looking at the concept of Local Governance.
Local governance in India, often called the "third tier" of government, was
institutionalized through major constitutional changes in 1992. It is divided
into Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) for rural areas and Municipalities
for urban areas. Local governance refers to decentralized administration at
the grassroots level aimed at - Democratic participation; Efficient service
delivery; Inclusive development and Accountability and transparency. In the
Pre-Constitution times it was mentioned in ancient texts as Rig Vedic
Sabha & Samiti. We know that there were also Village communities during
the Mauryan period.
Importance of PRIs is that they strengthen Grassroots democracy;
Participatory development; Political empowerment of women & SC/ST;
Better local planning; and Leadership development.
4
Challenges with local governance have been Inadequate financial
resources; Weak administrative capacity; Political interference; Partial
devolution of powers; and Low awareness among citizens.
Recent initiatives in India which have added value to Local Governance are
the e-Gram Swaraj Portal, SVAMITVA Scheme (property rights); Social
Audit mechanisms; and Digital Panchayats.
1. Constitutional Status & Framework
Initially, local government was only mentioned as a Directive Principle
under Article 40, which urged states to organize village panchayats. It
became a mandatory constitutional obligation through two landmark
amendments:
● 73rd Amendment Act (1992): Added Part IX ("The Panchayats")
and the Eleventh Schedule to the Constitution.
● 74th Amendment Act (1992): Added Part IX-A ("The Municipalities")
and the Twelfth Schedule.
● Seventh Schedule: Local government remains a State Subject
(Entry 5 of List II), meaning state legislatures have the power to enact
laws regarding their structure and functions.
2. Rural Local Governance (Panchayati Raj)
The Constitution mandates a three-tier structure in every state (except
those with a population under 20 lakh):
a) Gram Panchayat: Village level (Directly elected). The Gram
Panchayat is the primary executive body of village self-government
and the lowest level of the three-tier Panchayati Raj system. It is
5
directly accountable to the Gram Sabha, which consists of all
registered voters in the village.
The Gram Panchayat is composed of elected representatives who
serve a five-year term.
● Sarpanch (President): The head of the Panchayat, elected directly
by the villagers or indirectly by the Panchs, depending on state law.
● Panchs (Ward Members): The village is divided into wards, and
each ward elects a representative directly.
● Panchayat Secretary: A non-elected official appointed by the state
government to oversee administrative activities, maintain records,
and call meetings
●
b) Panchayat Samiti: The Panchayat Samiti is the intermediate
(middle) tier of the Panchayati Raj system in India. It operates at the
Block, Tehsil, or Taluka level and serves as the vital link between
the village-level Gram Panchayats and the district-level Zila Parishad.
The structure of a Panchayat Samiti varies slightly by state but
generally includes:
● Directly Elected Members: People chosen through direct elections
from territorial constituencies within the block.
● Ex-officio Members: All Sarpanches (heads) of the Gram
Panchayats within the block.
● Legislators: Members of Parliament (MPs) and Members of the
Legislative Assembly (MLAs) representing that block.
6
● Leadership: The members elect a Chairperson (often called
Pradhan or Sabhapati) and a Vice-Chairperson from among
themselves.
● Administrative Head: The Block Development Officer (BDO) acts
as the executive officer and is appointed by the state government.
The Panchayat Samiti acts as a coordinating and supervisory body:
● Planning: It collects development plans from all Gram Panchayats in
its jurisdiction and consolidates them into a unified Block Plan.
● Execution: It implements specific schemes related to agriculture,
social forestry, primary health care, and rural water supply.
● Supervision: It examines the budgets of the Gram Panchayats and
supervises their overall functioning.
● Resource Allocation: It distributes funds and grants received from
the Zila Parishad or the state government to the respective Gram
Panchayats
While commonly called the Panchayat Samiti, it is known by different
names across India: Mandal Parishad: Andhra Pradesh &
Telangana; Taluka Panchayat: Gujarat; Anchalik Panchayat:
Assam; Kshetra Panchayat: Uttar Pradesh
c) Zilla Parishad: The Zila Parishad (District Council) is the highest tier
of the Panchayati Raj system in India, operating at the District level. It
serves as a bridge between the state government and the lower-tier
7
local bodies (Gram Panchayats and Panchayat Samitis), ensuring
that rural development is coordinated across the entire district.
The Zila Parishad is a "body corporate" with a fixed 5-year term. Its
members include:
● Directly Elected Members: Representatives elected from territorial
constituencies within the district (typically 50–75 members depending
on population).
● Ex-officio Members: All Chairpersons of the Panchayat Samitis
(block level) in the district.
● Legislative Representatives: Local MPs (Members of Parliament)
and MLAs (Members of Legislative Assembly) whose constituencies
fall within the district.
● Leadership: An elected President (Adhyaksha) and Vice-President.
● Administrative Head: A Chief Executive Officer (CEO), usually an
IAS officer or senior state official, who heads the administrative wing.
The Zila Parishad acts primarily as a coordinating, supervisory, and
advisory body: It takes care of -
● District Planning: It consolidates the plans prepared by Panchayat
Samitis to create a unified District Development Plan.
● Supervision: It oversees the activities of Gram Panchayats and
Panchayat Samitis and, in some states, scrutinises their budgets.
● Developmental Works: It implements schemes related to minor
irrigation, social forestry, primary health, and vocational education.
8
● Financial Management: It distributes funds and grants from the state
and central governments to the lower-tier panchayats.
● Advisory Role: It advises the state government on all developmental
needs and matters related to the district's rural population.
3. Urban Local Governance (Municipalities)
The 74th Amendment provides for three types of urban bodies based on
population size:
● Nagar Panchayat: For transitional areas (rural to urban).
● Municipal Council: For smaller urban areas.
● Municipal Corporation: For larger urban areas (e.g., metros).
● Functions: The Twelfth Schedule contains 18 functional items,
including urban planning, water supply, and public health.
4.Key Features of Local Governance
- DURATION - Fixed 5-year term; elections must be held within 6
months if dissolved early.
- RESERVATION - Mandatory 1/3rd reservation for women; seats for
SC/ST in proportion to population
- ELECTIONS - Conducted by an independent State Election
Commission (SEC)
- FINANCE - State Finance Commissions (SFC) are set up every 5
years to review the financial position.
- PLANNING - District Planning Committees (DPC) consolidate
plans from both rural and urban bodies
5. Special Provisions (PESA Act)
9

protect customary laws and traditional management of resources.

Role of Chief Minister and Council of Minister
Welcome back learners! My name is Amrita and today let me begin with a question to you
Who actually runs the state?
Imagine your state as a giant open-world game map. There are missions, side quests,
emergencies, upgrades, limited resources, and millions of players expecting the system to run
smoothly. Now someone has to make the big decisions, assign roles, manage the chaos, and keep
the game from crashing. In real life, that “game master” is the Chief Minister, and the team
running the game is the Council of Ministers. Together, they are the real executive authority of
the state government.
Most people assume the Governor runs the state because the Governor is the constitutional head.
But the actual decision-making power lies elsewhere. The Governor is more like the ceremonial
figure who ensures rules are followed, while the Chief Minister and the Council of Ministers are
the ones actively steering governance. Whenever a new scheme is announced, a policy is
introduced, or emergency action is taken, the decisions are coming from this executive
leadership.
The constitutional foundation of this system comes from Articles 163, 164, 166 and 167 of the
Indian Constitution. These provisions establish that the Governor acts on the aid and advice of
the Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister. This is part of India’s parliamentary
system. Just as the Prime Minister runs the Union government, the Chief Minister runs the state
government. The structure is almost identical, only the level of governance changes.
So how does someone become the Chief Minister? The Governor appoints the Chief Minister,
but this is not a random choice. The leader of the party or coalition that has the majority in the
Legislative Assembly becomes the Chief Minister. Think of it like a popularity plus numbers
game. If a party wins enough seats, its leader gets the top executive role.
Things get interesting when no party has a clear majority. This is called a hung assembly, and it
is like a suspense episode in a political series. Alliances form, negotiations happen, and the
Governor may invite the largest party or coalition to form the government. The final test is the
floor test in the Assembly, where the Chief Minister must prove they actually have majority
support. You may have seen news flashes about midnight meetings, oath ceremonies, and
dramatic voting days. All of that revolves around who gets to become Chief Minister.
The Chief Minister must be a citizen of India and a member of the state legislature. If they are
not already a member at the time of appointment, they have six months to get elected. Otherwise,
they must step down. This ensures that the person leading the state has democratic legitimacy.
Once appointed, the Chief Minister becomes the centre of executive power. One of their biggest
responsibilities is forming the Council of Ministers. They choose who becomes a minister,
decide which department each minister handles, and can reshuffle roles whenever necessary. If
governance were a team project, the Chief Minister would be the one assigning tasks, reviewing
performance, and changing team members if things go wrong.
For example, if a minister fails to manage a department or gets caught in controversy, the Chief
Minister may ask them to resign. This is not just politics; it is about maintaining administrative
credibility. The Chief Minister also acts as the bridge between the Governor and the Council of
Ministers, communicating decisions, policies, and developments.
The role of the Chief Minister goes beyond coordination. They are the chief policy leader of the
state. When a government introduces free bus travel, farmer support schemes, startup policies,
health insurance programmes, or digital governance platforms, these decisions emerge from
executive planning led by the Chief Minister and the cabinet. The Chief Minister also guides the
legislative agenda, deciding what bills will be introduced and defended in the Assembly.
The Chief Minister is also the main face of the government inside the Legislative Assembly.
During debates, they respond to criticism, defend policies, and justify decisions. This makes the
role politically demanding and highly visible. During crises, the importance of this leadership
becomes even clearer. Whether it is floods, pandemics, or law-and-order breakdowns, the Chief
Minister coordinates departments, resources, and responses. During the COVID-19 pandemic,
for example, state governments handled lockdowns, healthcare coordination, oxygen supply, and
vaccination planning under the leadership of Chief Ministers.
Working with the Chief Minister is the Council of Ministers, which functions like the core
leadership team of the state. This includes Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State, and sometimes
Deputy Ministers. Cabinet Ministers handle major departments such as finance, home affairs,
health, and education. Ministers of State assist them and may also handle independent
responsibilities.
The most important principle guiding this body is collective responsibility. This means the
Council of Ministers functions as one united team accountable to the Legislative Assembly. If
the Assembly passes a no-confidence motion, the entire Council must resign. Even if ministers
disagree during internal discussions, they must support cabinet decisions publicly. Governance
cannot run like a group chat where everyone has a different opinion. It must operate with one
clear official position.
At the same time, ministers have individual responsibility for their departments. A health
minister must answer for hospital systems, an education minister for exam management, and a
transport minister for public mobility. If something goes wrong, they are held accountable. The
Chief Minister monitors performance and can make changes when necessary.
The relationship between the Governor and the Council of Ministers is also important. Normally,
the Governor acts on the advice of the Council. However, there are certain situations where the
Governor has discretionary powers, such as inviting a party to form the government in a hung
assembly or reserving a bill for the President’s consideration. Even then, routine governance is
carried out by the elected executive.
Decision-making inside the government follows a structured process. A proposal often begins at
the department level, where bureaucrats prepare plans and drafts. The concerned minister
reviews it, and the cabinet discusses it collectively. The Chief Minister plays a key role in
guiding the final decision. If a law is needed, it is introduced in the Assembly. Once approved,
the bureaucracy implements it. This shows how the political executive and administrative
machinery work together.
The legislative role of the Chief Minister and Council of Ministers is crucial. They introduce
most bills, shape policy priorities, and present the state budget. Laws on education, policing,
public health, and urban governance are often driven by the executive. The budget presented by
the Finance Minister reflects the priorities set by the cabinet under the leadership of the Chief
Minister.
Administratively, the Council supervises civil services, police forces, and district administration.
Officers at various levels operate within a framework guided by executive decisions. Financially,
the Council determines spending priorities, taxation proposals, welfare schemes, and
infrastructure investments. Every major development project or subsidy programme reflects
executive planning.
The Chief Minister also plays a political role beyond administration. They are often the face of
the ruling party in the state, leading campaigns and communicating with the public. Many voters
associate governance quality with the Chief Minister’s leadership style. This makes the role both
administrative and symbolic.
Real-life examples make this clearer. During floods, the Chief Minister leads emergency
coordination, ministers manage relief operations, and officials execute rescue work. During
law-and-order crises, decisions about curfews and policing strategies are taken at the executive
level. When education reforms are introduced, the department drafts proposals, the minister
presents them, and the cabinet approves them before implementation. Governance is essentially
teamwork led by executive authority.
Despite their power, the Chief Minister and Council of Ministers are not above accountability.
The Legislative Assembly questions them through debates and motions. Courts can strike down
unconstitutional decisions. Media scrutiny shapes public opinion. Ultimately, elections determine
whether the executive continues or is replaced.
The Chief Minister may leave office if they resign, lose majority support, or fail to win a
confidence vote. When the Chief Minister steps down, the entire Council of Ministers must also
resign because their authority comes from that leadership. This reflects the interconnected
structure of executive power.
The role of the Chief Minister at the state level closely mirrors that of the Prime Minister at the
national level. Both lead executive governance, both depend on legislative majority, and both
operate through collective responsibility. The difference lies only in scale.
Understanding this system makes everyday governance easier to decode. When a state launches
free Wi-Fi, announces job schemes, reforms school curriculum, or implements welfare policies,
the decisions are not random. They emerge from cabinet discussions, executive authority, and
constitutional procedures led by the Chief Minister and Council of Ministers.
In simple terms, the Chief Minister is the driver of the state government, and the Council of
Ministers is the engine powering it. Together, they transform political promises into policies and
policies into real-world governance. Every announcement, reform, and crisis response carries
their imprint. When news reports say, “The state government has decided,” what it really means
is that the Chief Minister and the Council of Ministers have taken that decision and set the
machinery of governance into motion.

In this lecture, we will discuss one of the most important constitutional authorities within the
State Legislature — the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker of the Legislative Assembly.
To understand parliamentary democracy at the state level, it is essential to understand the role
of these presiding officers. They are not merely moderators of debate. They are guardians of
legislative dignity, procedure, and constitutional order.
In a parliamentary democracy, procedure is not a mere technicality — it is the framework
through which democracy survives.
At the state level in India, this framework is protected and guided by two crucial constitutional
authorities: the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker of the Legislative Assembly.
To understand how a State Legislature functions with order, discipline, and constitutional
balance, we must understand these two offices in depth.
Let us begin with the Speaker.
Under Article 178 of the Constitution of India, every Legislative Assembly shall, as soon as
possible, choose two of its members to be respectively the Speaker and the Deputy Speaker.
The Speaker is therefore not an external authority. The Speaker is elected from among the
elected representatives themselves.
Typically, the ruling party nominates a candidate for the position. However, once elected, the
Speaker is expected to function in an impartial and non-partisan manner. This expectation of
neutrality is not merely political courtesy — it is the moral foundation of parliamentary
democracy.
The Speaker presides over the meetings of the Legislative Assembly. No business can be
conducted in the House without the Speaker or, in their absence, the Deputy Speaker. In this
sense, the Speaker is the constitutional gatekeeper of legislative functioning.
One of the most visible roles of the Speaker is maintaining order and decorum in the House.
Legislative debates can be intense. Political disagreements can escalate. In such moments, the
Speaker ensures discipline. The Speaker can call members to order, suspend members for
misconduct, and even adjourn the House if necessary. This authority safeguards the dignity of
the Assembly.
The Speaker also controls the flow of debate. Who speaks? For how long? Which motions are
admissible? These decisions lie within the Speaker’s authority. In a House where multiple
parties compete for voice and visibility, this power becomes critical in ensuring fairness and
balance.
Another highly significant constitutional function of the Speaker relates to Money Bills. The
Speaker decides whether a bill introduced in the Assembly is a Money Bill. This decision is final.
In states that have a Legislative Council, this certification becomes especially important
because the Council has limited powers over Money Bills.
The Speaker also plays a pivotal role under the Tenth Schedule — the Anti-Defection Law.
Questions relating to the disqualification of members on grounds of defection are decided by the
Speaker. Although such decisions are subject to judicial review, the initial authority rests with the
Speaker. This function has made the office both powerful and politically sensitive in
contemporary times.
The Speaker is also the administrative head of the Assembly Secretariat and represents the
Legislative Assembly in its relations with the Governor and other authorities.
In terms of tenure, the Speaker remains in office from the date of election until just before the
first meeting of the next Assembly. Even if the Assembly is dissolved, the Speaker continues
until a new Speaker is elected. The Speaker may resign by writing to the Deputy Speaker and
may be removed by a resolution of the Assembly passed by a majority of all the then members,
provided due notice is given.
Thus, the Speaker is not merely a moderator of debate. The Speaker is the custodian of
legislative procedure, authority, and institutional dignity.
Now let us turn to the Deputy Speaker.
Like the Speaker, the Deputy Speaker is elected by the members of the Legislative Assembly
under Article 178. The Constitution does not mandate political affiliation. However, in many
states, by convention, the Deputy Speaker’s post is sometimes offered to a member of the
opposition to strengthen democratic balance.
The primary function of the Deputy Speaker is to preside over the House in the absence of the
Speaker. However, this is not a limited role. When presiding, the Deputy Speaker exercises all
the powers of the Speaker. The authority to maintain order, regulate debate, and conduct voting
applies equally when the Deputy Speaker occupies the Chair.
If the office of the Speaker falls vacant due to resignation, removal, or death, the Deputy
Speaker performs the duties of the Speaker until a new Speaker is elected. This ensures
continuity in legislative functioning. Parliamentary democracy cannot afford procedural vacuum,
and the Deputy Speaker prevents such institutional gaps.
The Deputy Speaker may resign by writing to the Speaker and may also be removed through a
resolution passed by the Assembly with prior notice.
When we compare the two offices, certain similarities become evident. Both are elected by the
Legislative Assembly. Both must be members of the Assembly. Both can be removed by a
resolution of the House. Both are expected to function impartially while presiding. Both derive
their authority directly from the Constitution.
However, important differences also exist.
The Speaker is the principal presiding officer of the Assembly. The Deputy Speaker acts
primarily in the Speaker’s absence. The power to certify a Money Bill rests with the Speaker.
Decisions under the Anti-Defection Law are ordinarily exercised by the Speaker. The Speaker
represents the Assembly institutionally and symbolically in external relations.
The Deputy Speaker’s authority becomes fully operational when presiding, but structurally, the
office is secondary to that of the Speaker.
In contemporary politics, the offices of Speaker and Deputy Speaker have gained renewed
importance. In situations involving coalition governments, floor tests, disqualification disputes,
and legislative disruptions, the decisions of the Speaker often shape political outcomes. Courts
have examined the limits of these powers, especially in cases related to defection.
Thus, while constitutionally designed as neutral arbiters, Speakers operate within politically
dynamic environments. The tension between impartiality and political affiliation remains a
subject of academic and judicial debate.
At this point, let us pause and move beyond theory.
Constitutional offices truly reveal their character not in textbooks, but in moments of political
stress.
To understand the moral weight of the Speaker’s office, let us begin with a story from the early
years of Indian democracy.
G.V. Mavalankar, the first Speaker of the Lok Sabha, set a powerful precedent for what the
office was meant to represent. When he assumed office, he made it clear that the Speaker does
not belong to a political party anymore — the Speaker belongs to the House.
He believed that the moment a member is elected as Speaker, they must detach themselves
from partisan loyalties. The Chair, according to him, symbolizes the collective dignity of the
legislature.
There is an anecdote often associated with his functioning: even when members from his own
political background were involved in heated debates, he ruled strictly on procedural grounds,
never on political convenience.
His conduct established a moral principle — that impartiality is not merely expected of the
Speaker; it is the essence of the office.
This idea shaped parliamentary culture for decades. It created an expectation that the Speaker
would rise above party lines and protect the integrity of debate.
Did you know ?
In 2016, the State of Arunachal Pradesh witnessed a dramatic constitutional crisis. Internal
conflicts within the ruling party led to political instability. The Speaker of the Legislative
Assembly disqualified several MLAs under the Anti-Defection Law.
Simultaneously, the Deputy Speaker issued a notice seeking the removal of the Speaker.
What followed was a constitutional confrontation.
Questions emerged:
Can a Speaker facing removal continue to exercise disqualification powers?
Can legislative procedure be altered during a political crisis?
Where does constitutional morality stand in such situations?
The matter eventually reached the Supreme Court. In a landmark judgment, the Court restored
the dismissed government and emphasized the importance of constitutional process and
procedural integrity.
This incident demonstrated something crucial.
The Speaker’s office is not merely ceremonial. It can influence the survival of governments. It
operates at the intersection of law and politics. And in times of instability, its decisions carry
enormous constitutional consequences.
When we place the example of Mavalankar alongside the Arunachal Pradesh crisis, a powerful
contrast emerges.
On one side, we see the ideal — the Speaker as a neutral guardian of democratic dignity.
On the other side, we see the challenge — the Speaker functioning within intense political
conflict, where neutrality itself is questioned.
This tension is not a weakness of the Constitution. It is the reality of democratic governance.
The Constitution expects impartiality. Political circumstances test it.
And that is precisely why the offices of Speaker and Deputy Speaker are so vital. They are not
just managers of debate. They are custodians of constitutional trust.
Ultimately, the strength of parliamentary democracy depends not only on written provisions, but
on the integrity with which constitutional offices are exercised.
And in that sense, the Chair of the Speaker is not simply a seat in the Assembly — it is a
symbol of democratic responsibility.
Ultimately, the Speaker and Deputy Speaker are pillars of legislative democracy at the state
level. They ensure that debate does not descend into disorder, that procedure guides politics,
and that constitutional norms are preserved within the Assembly.
Democracy is not sustained only through elections. It is sustained through rules, institutions,
and restraint.
And at the centre of that institutional restraint stands the Speaker — supported by the Deputy
Speaker — ensuring that the voice of the people is expressed within constitutional boundaries.

Debates on State Legislatures in India
State Legislatures are the law-making bodies of India's states and are a vital
component of the country's federal democratic system. Constituted under
Part VI (Articles 168–212) of the Constitution, they legislate on subjects in
the State List and Concurrent List, approve state budgets, and hold the
executive accountable. Despite their constitutional importance, the
functioning, powers, and effectiveness of State Legislatures have been the
subject of extensive academic, political, and constitutional debate.
1. Constituent Assembly Debates (1946–1949)
During the drafting of the Indian Constitution, the framers engaged in intense
debates on how state-level democracy should be structured:
Bicameralism vs. Unicameralism (Vidhan Parishads): Prominent
members like K.T. Shah argued against creating Legislative Councils
(Vidhan Parishads) at the state level. They viewed second chambers as
expensive, undemocratic, and unnecessary "stumbling blocks" that could
delay progressive legislation. Others argued that upper houses provided a
stabilizing influence and allowed experts or seasoned statesmen to
contribute without facing direct elections.
Arguments in Favour of Bicameralism - was to make Second Chamber
as a Revising House. Supporters argued that a Legislative Council (Vidhan
Parishad) would act as a revising chamber, carefully examining Bills passed
by the Legislative Assembly. This would prevents hasty legislation, correct
drafting errors and encourages thoughtful deliberation. N. Gopalaswami
Ayyangar argued that a second chamber would moderate legislative
excesses and improve the quality of law-making.
It was argued that bicameralism would provide representation of
Special Interests. Legislative Councils were designed to include
2
representatives of Local bodies, Teachers, Graduates and Persons with
specialised knowledge. Supporters believed this would enrich legislative
discussions by bringing expertise into governance.
There would be continuity and stability. Unlike Legislative Assemblies,
Legislative Councils are permanent bodies with one-third of their members
retiring every two years. Advantages include - Institutional continuity,
Stability during political changes and Preservation of legislative experience.
There would be Protection Against Majoritarianism. A second chamber
was viewed as a safeguard against temporary political majorities.It could:
Delay controversial legislation - Encourage reconsideration - and Promote
consensus-building. It was argued that there would be better legislative
deliberation. Supporters argued that democracy required not merely
majority rule but informed discussion. The Council would improve debate,
reduce impulsive decision and strengthen legislative scrutiny.
Arguments Against Bicameralism were that it would cause
unnecessary delay. Many members argued that Legislative Councils
merely delayed legislation without having real authority. Since the Assembly
ultimately prevailed in most cases, critics believed the Council added little
value. They argued that a second house would add financial burden as
maintaining a second House required salaries, infrastructure, administrative
staff, elections and other operational costs. Critics questioned whether newly
independent states could justify this expenditure. It was also accused to have
anti-democratic character. Unlike Legislative Assemblies, Legislative
Councils are not directly elected by the entire electorate. Some members
argued that democratic legitimacy rested with the popularly elected
Assembly and indirect elections weakened accountability. Several members
feared that governments would use Legislative Councils to accommodate
defeated politicians, reward political supporters and expand political
patronage. This criticism remains relevant in contemporary debates. There
was also the fear that it would lead to duplication of work. Opponents
argued that both Houses would discuss the same Bills, leading to repetition,
3
delay and administrative inefficiency. A single House, they argued, would
legislate more efficiently.
On this issue let’s look at the views of key members of the Constituent
assembly. Dr B. R. Ambedkar did not insist that every state should have two
Houses. He favoured flexibility rather than constitutional compulsion and
supported allowing states to decide whether they needed a Legislative
Council. N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar - strongly defended bicameralism,
considered the Legislative Council a revising and deliberative body and
emphasised careful scrutiny of legislation rather than obstruction. Lokanath
Misra supported second chambers as institutions that could provide mature
and reflective legislative discussion. Mohammad Tahir expressed concerns
about the practical utility and democratic value of Legislative Councils and
favoured simpler legislative structures in many states.
The Constitutional Compromise was that the Constituent Assembly
rejected both extremes. It did not make bicameralism compulsory for all
states. It did not abolish Legislative Councils altogether. Instead, it
adopted a flexible federal model.
Article 168 - Provides that states may have a Governor and one Legislative
House (Legislative Assembly), or a Governor and two Houses (Legislative
Assembly and Legislative Council).
Article 169 - Allows Parliament to create or abolish a Legislative Council if
the concerned State Legislative Assembly passes a resolution by majority of
the total membership, and a majority of not less than two-thirds of the
members present and voting. This arrangement allows states to adapt their
legislative structure to changing political and administrative needs.
Legislative Autonomy and Federal Balance: Federalists argued for
maximum financial and policy autonomy for state assemblies under the State
List. However, in the wake of Partition, national unity was prioritized, resulting
in provisions that allow the Central Government or Parliament to override

state subjects under specific circumstances (e.g., Article 249, Emergency

In recent decades, debate has shifted toward the operational health,
effectiveness, and constitutional role of State Legislative Assemblies (Vidhan

A. The Decline in Sitting Days
A central debate centers on the declining duration of assembly sessions
across states.

Legislative Research), state assemblies meet for an average of just 20 to 25
days per year, with a significant portion of business packed into short Budget

The Impact: Short sessions severely limit time for Opposition questions,
private members' business, and scrutiny of state budgets. Reforms often
propose enforcing a constitutional minimum (e.g., 60–90 days annually
depending on state size, as recommended by the NCRWC).
B. Hasty Lawmaking and Lack of Committee Scrutiny
Unlike the Parliament of India—which relies heavily on Department-related
Standing Committees—most state legislatures lack robust standing
committee systems to scrutinize draft bills.
-A vast majority of state-level bills are introduced and passed on the same
day or within 24–48 hours with minimal debate.
-Critics argue that bypassing public feedback and detailed committee review
leads to poor legislation and frequent judicial challenges.
C. The Role of the Governor vs. State Assemblies
The tension between the Governor and the State Assembly (Vidhan
Sabha) is one of the most prominent flashpoints in Indian federalism.
9
While the Governor is formally the constitutional head of the state, state
governments operate under a parliamentary system where real executive
authority rests with the elected Council of Ministers, led by the Chief Minister.
Tensions arise because the Governor is appointed by the Central
Government, often leading to conflicts of interest when different political
parties rule at the national and state levels. Thus the dynamic between state
governments/legislatures and gubernatorial offices is one of the most fiery
constitutional debates in modern Indian federalism.
Constitutional Role: Nominal Head vs. Elected Representatives - Under
Article 163 of the Indian Constitution, the Governor is required to exercise
their functions on the "aid and advice" of the Council of Ministers, except in
areas where the Constitution explicitly grants them discretionary powers.
The Supreme Court established in Samsher Singh v. State of Punjab
(1974) that the Governor is a formal, constitutional figurehead. They do not
possess an independent executive power that can override the elected
cabinet.
Key Areas of Conflict - A. Assent to Bills (Article 200)
When the State Assembly passes a bill, it goes to the Governor under Article
200, which presents three primary options:
1. Grant Assent.
2. Withhold Assent and return the bill with a message requesting
reconsideration.
3. Reserve the bill for the consideration of the President.
The conflict happens when governors in several states have held onto
bills passed by elected assemblies for months or years without taking
action (a tactic known as a "Pocket Veto").
The Judicial Landmark (State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor, 2025):
Addressing indefinite delays by Governors, the Supreme Court ruled that a
Governor cannot exercise an absolute or pocket veto. The Court held
that if a Governor withholds assent, they must return the bill to the
10
Assembly. If the Assembly re-passes the bill, the Governor is
constitutionally bound to grant assent and cannot retroactively reserve it for
the President. The Court also set judicial timeframes (typically 1 to 3
months) for action.
The next point is with regard to Summoning, Proroguing, and Dissolving the
Assembly. Under Article 174, the Governor summons the legislative house.
The Conflict happens when questions arise over whether a Governor can
refuse to convene the Assembly when requested by the Cabinet, or
conversely, force a floor test. What does the law state? In Nabam Rebia
v. Deputy Speaker (2016), the Supreme Court ruled that the Governor can
summon or prorogue the House only on the aid and advice of the Cabinet.
The Governor has discretionary power to order a floor test only if there is
objective material showing the government has lost its majority.
Role as University Chancellors - In many states, the Governor acts as exofficio Chancellor of state-run universities.The Conflict is: Governors have
clashed with state governments over the appointment of Vice-Chancellors
(VCs). State Assemblies have frequently passed bills to remove the
Governor as Chancellor and replace them with the Chief Minister or an
eminent academic—leading Governors to stall those very bills.
Recommending President's Rule (Article 356) - Historically, Governors
have been accused of acting as agents of the Union government by
sending reports recommending the dismissal of state governments and
imposing President's Rule under Article 356. The Safeguard: In S.R.
Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court held that the
Governor's report under Article 356 is subject to judicial review, and the
strength of a ministry must be tested only on the floor of the House, not in
the Governor's private assessment.
D. Anti-Defection Law & Disqualification Powers
In India, there is no separate "State Parliament"—the laws governing state
assemblies (Vidhan Sabhas and Vidhan Parishads) are integrated directly
11
into the Indian Constitution. The Anti-Defection Law (enacted via the
Tenth Schedule by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment in 1985) applies
equally to both the national Parliament and all State Legislatures, grants the
Assembly Speaker the authority to decide on disqualification petitions.
At the state level, the key constitutional mechanisms, recent controversies,
and debates regarding the Anti-Defection Law function as follows:
While Articles 101 and 102 apply anti-defection provisions to MPs in
Parliament, Articles 190 and 191(2) apply the exact same Tenth Schedule
rules to State MLAs and MLCs.
Under the Tenth Schedule, a state legislator faces disqualification if:
1. Voluntary Resignation/Conduct: They voluntarily give up their
political party's membership. (Courts have ruled this includes actions
outside the house, such as supporting an opposition party).
2. Defying the Party Whip: They vote or abstain from voting contrary to
the party's official direction (whip) without prior permission.
3. Independents & Nominated Members: An independent MLA joins a
political party post-election, or a nominated member joins a party
after 6 months.
The 2/3rd Merger Exception - Disqualification is avoided only if at least twothirds (2/3rd) of the members of a legislative party agree to merge with
another political party. (The earlier provision allowing a "split" by 1/3rd of
members was abolished in 2003 via the 91st Amendment).
Over the past decade, state politics in India (such as in Karnataka, Madhya
Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Goa) has seen political parties find loopholes in
the Tenth Schedule:
● Mass Resignations ("Operation Lotus" Strategy): Instead of
directly defecting to another party—which would trigger
disqualification—MLAs resign from their seats entirely. This reduces
the overall strength of the House, bringing down the majority mark
12
and causing the ruling government to fall. The resigned MLAs then
run in the resulting by-elections under the new party’s ticket.
● The Speaker's Delay ("Pocket Veto"): Because the law does not
prescribe a strict deadline for deciding on disqualification petitions,
State Speakers (who often hail from the ruling party) sit on petitions
indefinitely. By the time a decision is made, the assembly's 5-year
term is often nearly over.
In state assemblies, the Speaker acts as a judicial tribunal to decide on
disqualification petitions. However, because the Speaker remains affiliated
with their political party, their neutrality is frequently challenged in court.
● Judicial Review (Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu, 1992): The
Supreme Court ruled that while the Speaker makes the initial
decision, their ruling is a tribunal order and is fully subject to judicial
review by High Courts and the Supreme Court.
● Setting a Deadline (Keisham Meghachandra Singh, 2020): The
Supreme Court held that State Speakers should ideally decide
disqualification petitions within 3 months, barring extraordinary
circumstances.
● Speaker Facing Removal (Subhash Desai / Maharashtra Crisis,
2023): The Supreme Court analyzed how rebel factions send notices
to remove the Speaker to prevent the Speaker from disqualifying
them under the Tenth Schedule, affirming that legislative party
actions cannot override the organizational structure of the core
political party.
To stop the recurring instability in state governments, major constitutional
committees have proposed key changes:
1. Independent Tribunal: Moving the power to decide disqualification
away from the Speaker to an independent permanent tribunal headed
by a retired judge (Supreme Court recommendation) or to the
Governor acting on the binding advice of the Election Commission
(Dinesh Goswami Committee / Law Commission).
13
2. Restricting Whips: Amending the law so that party whips apply only
to critical votes affecting government stability (like No-Confidence
Motions, Budget/Money Bills), leaving legislators free to vote their
conscience on ordinary bills.
3. Barring Re-election: Mandating that any MLA who defects or
resigns to alter government majorities be barred from contesting
elections or holding remunerative political office for the remainder of
that assembly's term.
Major Reform Suggestions
● Increase the number of annual sitting days.
● Strengthen Question Hour and Zero Hour.
● Make committee scrutiny mandatory for significant Bills.
● Enhance research and technical support for MLAs.
● Improve transparency through digital legislative platforms.
● Clarify the constitutional role of Governors.
● Encourage wider public participation in the legislative process.
● Review the functioning and utility of Legislative Councils.
● Promote greater representation of women and marginalised groups.
● Improve financial oversight through stronger Public Accounts and
Estimates Committees.
Conclusion
The debates surrounding State Legislatures reflect broader questions about
federalism, democratic accountability, legislative efficiency, and
constitutional governance in India. While State Legislatures remain the
principal institutions for representing regional interests and making state
laws, concerns over declining deliberation, executive dominance,
centralisation, and institutional effectiveness have prompted calls for
comprehensive reforms. Strengthening these legislatures is essential for
ensuring cooperative federalism, responsive governance, and a vibrant
democratic system.

a) Local Governance & Panchayati Raj Institutions
Let us begin by first looking at the concept of Local Governance.
Local governance in India, often called the "third tier" of government,
was institutionalized through major constitutional changes in 1992. It
is divided into Panchayati Raj Institutions (PRIs) for rural areas and
Municipalities for urban areas. Local governance refers to
decentralized administration at the grassroots level aimed at -
Democratic participation; Efficient service delivery; Inclusive
development and Accountability and transparency. In the
Pre-Constitution times it was mentioned in ancient texts as Rig Vedic
Sabha & Samiti. We know that there were also Village communities
during the Mauryan period.
Importance of PRIs is that they strengthen Grassroots democracy;
Participatory development; Political empowerment of women &
SC/ST; Better local planning; and Leadership development.
Challenges with local governance have been Inadequate financial
resources; Weak administrative capacity; Political interference; Partial
devolution of powers; and Low awareness among citizens.
Recent initiatives in India which have added value to Local
Governance are the e-Gram Swaraj Portal, SVAMITVA Scheme
(property rights); Social Audit mechanisms; and Digital Panchayats.
1. Constitutional Status & Framework
Initially, local government was only mentioned as a Directive Principle
under Article 40, which urged states to organize village panchayats. It
became a mandatory constitutional obligation through two landmark
amendments:
● 73rd Amendment Act (1992): Added Part IX ("The
Panchayats") and the Eleventh Schedule to the Constitution.
● 74th Amendment Act (1992): Added Part IX-A ("The
Municipalities") and the Twelfth Schedule.
● Seventh Schedule: Local government remains a State Subject
(Entry 5 of List II), meaning state legislatures have the power
to enact laws regarding their structure and functions.
2. Rural Local Governance (Panchayati Raj)
The Constitution mandates a three-tier structure in every state (except
those with a population under 20 lakh):
a) Gram Panchayat: Village level (Directly elected). The Gram
Panchayat is the primary executive body of village
self-government and the lowest level of the three-tier
Panchayati Raj system. It is directly accountable to the Gram
Sabha, which consists of all registered voters in the village.
The Gram Panchayat is composed of elected representatives who
serve a five-year term.
● Sarpanch (President): The head of the Panchayat, elected
directly by the villagers or indirectly by the Panchs, depending on
state law.
● Panchs (Ward Members): The village is divided into wards, and
each ward elects a representative directly.
● Panchayat Secretary: A non-elected official appointed by the
state government to oversee administrative activities, maintain
records, and call meetings
●
b) Panchayat Samiti: The Panchayat Samiti is the intermediate
(middle) tier of the Panchayati Raj system in India. It operates
at the Block, Tehsil, or Taluka level and serves as the vital link
between the village-level Gram Panchayats and the
district-level Zila Parishad.
The structure of a Panchayat Samiti varies slightly by state but
generally includes:
● Directly Elected Members: People chosen through direct
elections from territorial constituencies within the block.
● Ex-officio Members: All Sarpanches (heads) of the Gram
Panchayats within the block.
● Legislators: Members of Parliament (MPs) and Members of the
Legislative Assembly (MLAs) representing that block.
● Leadership: The members elect a Chairperson (often called
Pradhan or Sabhapati) and a Vice-Chairperson from among
themselves.
● Administrative Head: The Block Development Officer (BDO)
acts as the executive officer and is appointed by the state
government.
The Panchayat Samiti acts as a coordinating and supervisory body:
● Planning: It collects development plans from all Gram Panchayats
in its jurisdiction and consolidates them into a unified Block Plan.
● Execution: It implements specific schemes related to agriculture,
social forestry, primary health care, and rural water supply.
● Supervision: It examines the budgets of the Gram Panchayats
and supervises their overall functioning.
● Resource Allocation: It distributes funds and grants received
from the Zila Parishad or the state government to the respective
Gram Panchayats
While commonly called the Panchayat Samiti, it is known by different
names across India: Mandal Parishad: Andhra Pradesh &
Telangana; Taluka Panchayat: Gujarat; Anchalik Panchayat:
Assam; Kshetra Panchayat: Uttar Pradesh
c) Zilla Parishad: The Zila Parishad (District Council) is the
highest tier of the Panchayati Raj system in India, operating at
the District level. It serves as a bridge between the state
government and the lower-tier local bodies (Gram Panchayats
and Panchayat Samitis), ensuring that rural development is
coordinated across the entire district.
The Zila Parishad is a "body corporate" with a fixed 5-year term.
Its members include:
● Directly Elected Members: Representatives elected from
territorial constituencies within the district (typically 50–75
members depending on population).
● Ex-officio Members: All Chairpersons of the Panchayat
Samitis (block level) in the district.
● Legislative Representatives: Local MPs (Members of
Parliament) and MLAs (Members of Legislative Assembly)
whose constituencies fall within the district.
● Leadership: An elected President (Adhyaksha) and
Vice-President.
● Administrative Head: A Chief Executive Officer (CEO), usually
an IAS officer or senior state official, who heads the
administrative wing.
The Zila Parishad acts primarily as a coordinating, supervisory,
and advisory body: It takes care of -
● District Planning: It consolidates the plans prepared by
Panchayat Samitis to create a unified District Development
Plan.
● Supervision: It oversees the activities of Gram Panchayats
and Panchayat Samitis and, in some states, scrutinises their
budgets.
● Developmental Works: It implements schemes related to
minor irrigation, social forestry, primary health, and
vocational education.
● Financial Management: It distributes funds and grants from
the state and central governments to the lower-tier
panchayats.
● Advisory Role: It advises the state government on all
developmental needs and matters related to the district's rural
population.
3. Urban Local Governance (Municipalities)
The 74th Amendment provides for three types of urban bodies based
on population size:
● Nagar Panchayat: For transitional areas (rural to urban).
● Municipal Council: For smaller urban areas.
● Municipal Corporation: For larger urban areas (e.g., metros).
● Functions: The Twelfth Schedule contains 18 functional
items, including urban planning, water supply, and public
health.
4.Key Features of Local Governance
- DURATION - Fixed 5-year term; elections must be held within 6
months if dissolved early.
- RESERVATION - Mandatory 1/3rd reservation for women; seats
for SC/ST in proportion to population
- ELECTIONS - Conducted by an independent State Election
Commission (SEC)
- FINANCE - State Finance Commissions (SFC) are set up every
5 years to review the financial position.
- PLANNING - District Planning Committees (DPC) consolidate
plans from both rural and urban bodies
5. Special Provisions (PESA Act)
The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA)
extends Part IX to "Scheduled Areas" (tribal areas) with modifications
to protect customary laws and traditional management of resources.
Recent Status (2025–2026)
● Elections: Several states, including Maharashtra and
Arunachal Pradesh, have recently held or scheduled Zila
Parishad elections to ensure continuous democratic
governance at the grassroots level.
● Digital Integration: Many Zila Parishads, such as those in
Karnataka, have moved towards digital platforms like "Mahiti
Kanaja" for transparent service delivery and grievance
registration.

infrastructure, sanitation, housing, and environmental sustainability. The amendment also
introduced Ward Committees in larger cities to encourage citizen participation at the
neighbourhood level. Think of it as hyper-local governance for urban life.
Just like the 73rd Amendment, the 74th ensured regular elections, reservations for marginalised
groups and women, and financial mechanisms to support urban bodies. It also listed 18
functional areas—such as urban planning, public health, water supply, and slum
improvement—to be managed by municipalities.

Roles and Functions of Municipalities
Hey everyone! Welcome back. I am Amrita and in this module, we’re zooming into urban
governance and understanding the roles and functions of municipalities—the institutions that
literally run our cities and towns. From garbage collection and water supply to city planning and
public health, municipalities are where everyday governance actually happens.
In a country like India, where cities are expanding at lightning speed, municipalities are not just
administrative bodies—they’re the backbone of urban life. Their authority comes from the 74th
Constitutional Amendment, which gave urban local bodies constitutional recognition under the
Constitution of India. That means municipalities are not optional governance units; they are
essential democratic institutions.
So let’s break this down: if Parliament and state governments set the “big picture,” municipalities
handle the “day-to-day survival” of urban citizens.
First, what exactly are municipalities? They are institutions of self-government for urban areas,
including Municipal Corporations for large cities, Municipal Councils for medium towns, and
Nagar Panchayats for areas transitioning from rural to urban. Their goal is simple—manage
cities efficiently, democratically, and inclusively.
Now let’s talk about what they actually do.
One of the most important roles of municipalities is urban planning. Cities don’t just grow
randomly; they need structured development. Municipalities prepare master plans, regulate land
use, and approve building constructions. They decide where roads, parks, housing zones, and
commercial spaces will exist. Without this function, cities would turn into chaotic, unmanageable
spaces.
Connected to planning is infrastructure development. Municipalities are responsible for roads,
streetlights, drainage systems, and public transport support. When a pothole gets fixed, a new
flyover is built, or street lighting improves in your area—that’s municipal governance in action.
Water supply is another core function. Municipal bodies ensure access to clean drinking water,
manage pipelines, maintain water tankss, and address shortages. In urban areas, this is not just
a service; it’s a public health necessity.
Sanitation and waste management might sound basic, but they are among the most critical
municipal responsibilities. Garbage collection, sewage treatment, public toilets, and maintaining
cleanliness in public spaces all fall under municipalities. A city’s health depends heavily on how
efficiently these systems function.
Public health is another major area. Municipalities run urban health centres, vaccination
programmes, and disease-control campaigns. During outbreaks—like dengue, COVID-19, or
other communicable diseases—it is often the municipal machinery that responds first on the
ground.
Urban poverty alleviation is also a key function. Municipal bodies implement welfare schemes
related to housing, employment, and food security. Slum improvement projects, rehabilitation
programmes, and affordable housing initiatives are typically coordinated at the municipal level.
Education and social development are part of their responsibilities too. Many municipalities
manage primary schools, libraries, and community centres. They create spaces for learning,
skill development, and social engagement.
Environmental protection is becoming one of the most important municipal roles today. Urban
areas face pollution, deforestation, and climate-related risks. Municipalities work on urban
forestry, lake restoration, pollution control, and sustainable development projects. Smart cities
and green initiatives largely depend on municipal planning and implementation.
Another major function is regulation. Municipalities issue trade licenses, regulate markets,
monitor construction safety, and ensure compliance with urban laws. Whether it’s a restaurant
opening in your area or a shopping complex being built, municipal approval is essential.
They also play a key role in disaster management. Floods, fires, building collapses, and
heatwaves require quick response systems. Municipal authorities coordinate rescue operations,
relief services, and rehabilitation efforts at the local level.
Now here’s the democratic side of it. Municipalities are not just service providers—they are
political institutions. They are run by elected representatives like councillors and mayors.
Citizens vote for these leaders, who then make decisions on budgets, policies, and
development priorities. This makes municipalities a direct interface between citizens and
governance.
Participation is central. Ward committees and public consultations allow residents to voice
concerns about roads, water, safety, and infrastructure. If democracy at the national level feels
distant, municipal governance is where people can directly influence decisions.
Financial functions are equally important. Municipalities prepare budgets, collect taxes like
property tax, and allocate resources for development. They receive funds from state
governments and grants recommended by finance commissions. But they also generate their
own revenue, which gives them some degree of financial independence.
However, let’s keep it real—municipal governance is not without challenges. Many municipalities
struggle with limited funds, administrative constraints, and growing urban populations. Rapid
urbanisation means cities need more housing, transport, and services, but resources don’t
always keep up.
Political interference can also affect decision-making. Sometimes, municipalities depend heavily
on state governments for approvals and funding, which limits their autonomy. Administrative
capacity, lack of trained personnel, and outdated infrastructure can further slow down
implementation.
Despite these challenges, municipalities remain critical for urban democracy. They bring
governance closer to people and make it responsive. A national policy might exist on paper, but
it is the municipality that translates it into action on the ground.
Your daily life in a city—public transport, Wi-Fi zones, waste management, water supply, public
parks, safety lighting—everything connects back to municipal governance. Even digital
governance initiatives like online tax payments, grievance portals, and civic apps are run by
municipal systems.
Municipalities are also spaces where young leaders can emerge. Many political careers begin at
the local level. Councillors and mayors understand grassroots issues firsthand, which shapes
larger policy leadership later.
For learners on SWAYAM, understanding municipalities is not just about memorising
constitutional provisions—it’s about seeing governance in action. When you pay property tax,
attend a ward meeting, volunteer for a cleanliness drive, or raise a civic complaint, you’re
participating in municipal governance.
Another important role is inclusivity. Reservations for women, Scheduled Castes, and
Scheduled Tribes ensure representation in municipal bodies. This makes urban governance
more democratic and socially just. Women leaders, in particular, have played transformative
roles in improving sanitation, health, and education at the local level.
Technology is reshaping municipal functions too. Smart city initiatives, digital governance
platforms, surveillance systems for safety, and data-driven urban planning are redefining how
municipalities operate. Cities are increasingly using tech to improve efficiency, transparency,
and citizen engagement.
Climate change has added a new dimension. Urban floods, heatwaves, and pollution crises
demand local action. Municipalities now engage in climate-resilient planning, sustainable
mobility, renewable energy initiatives, and green infrastructure development.
So what’s the big takeaway?
Municipalities are not minor governance units. They are where democracy becomes practical,
visible, and immediate. They connect policies to people. They manage crises, deliver services,
and shape urban futures.
If governance were a game, municipalities would be the “live servers”—constantly running,
updating, and responding in real time.
This space matters more than ever. Urban populations are rising, civic awareness is growing,
and expectations from governance are changing. Citizens today want transparency, efficiency,
and participation. Municipalities are at the centre of this transformation.
So next time you see a road repair, a new metro station, a waste segregation campaign, or a
public health drive—remember, that’s municipal governance working behind the scenes.
Democracy doesn’t just sit in Parliament buildings. It lives in city offices, ward meetings,
neighbourhood projects, and community decisions. Municipalities make cities not just
livable—but governable.
And understanding their roles and functions is the first step toward becoming an informed,
engaged urban citizen.

Understanding the Structure of Indian Judiciary
The Indian Judiciary is designed as a single integrated system. Unlike
federal systems like the United States—where state and federal courts
operate independently—the Indian system is a hierarchy where the
Supreme Court sits at the apex, exercising authority over all lower courts.
Here is a breakdown of the structure, from the top down.
1. The Supreme Court of India (Apex Court)
Located in New Delhi, the Supreme Court is the highest constitutional court
and the final court of appeal.
Composition: Led by the Chief Justice of India (CJI) and a maximum of 33
other judges.
Key Powers: Original Jurisdiction: Resolves disputes between the Union
and States or between two States.
o Appellate Jurisdiction: Hears appeals against judgments
from High Courts.
o Advisory Jurisdiction: The President can seek its opinion
on matters of public importance.
o Guardian of the Constitution: It has the power of Judicial
Review to strike down laws that violate fundamental rights.
2. High Courts (State Level)
Each state (or group of states) has a High Court. Currently, there are 25
High Courts in India.
Jurisdiction: They exercise power over a State or a Union Territory.
Writ Jurisdiction: Under Article 226, they can issue writs (orders) to
enforce fundamental rights, often with a broader scope than the
Supreme Court.
· Supervisory Power: They supervise and control all courts and
tribunals within their territorial jurisdiction.
3. Subordinate Courts (District Level)
These courts handle the bulk of legal cases in India and are divided based
on the nature of the case: Civil or Criminal.
The District Court is presided over by the District Judge and the nature of
cases it handles are property, contracts, marraige/divorce, and inheritance.
The Sessions Court is presided over by the Sessions Judge and the nature
of cases that it looks into are serious crimes like murder, theft or assault.
4. Specialized Tribunals and Lok Adalats - To reduce the burden on the
main hierarchy, India uses specialized bodies:
Tribunals: Quasi-judicial bodies for specific areas, such as the National
Green Tribunal (NGT) or the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT).
Lok Adalats (People’s Courts): An alternative dispute resolution
mechanism where cases pending in courts are settled amicably to save
time and money
There are also Lower Courts
Lower courts, such Munsif's Courts and Small Causes Courts, function
beneath the District Courts in certain states. These courts deal with
small-scale civil claims and financial problems.
Jurisdiction: These courts can hear cases involving lesser claims and
usually have original jurisdiction. Civil courts in some states can handle
matters worth any amount of money since they have unrestricted pecuniary
jurisdiction.
Commercial Courts: Specialized Courts for Business Disputes
Established by the Commercial Courts Act of 2015, the Commercial Courts
exclusively handle business disputes involving sums over Rs. 3, 00,000.
The goal of these courts is to settle business-related matters more quickly.
• Jurisdiction and Process: In order to ensure speedy hearings and
verdicts in intricate business cases, Commercial Courts adhere to a more
stringent process than standard civil courts.
Tribunals: Specialized Forums for Expert Justice
In India, tribunals deal with certain legal fields, providing prompt justice and
specialized knowledge in areas such as administrative conflicts, corporate
law, and taxes. These organizations have more latitude in their processes
and function outside of the conventional judicial system.
Notable Courts: The Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT), Income Tax
Appellate Tribunal (ITAT), and National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) are
a few of the important tribunals. In order to relieve the load on ordinary
courts and guarantee that disagreements are settled by subject-matter
specialists, these tribunals were set up to handle specialty issues.
In addition to the aforementioned, alternative dispute resolution (ADR)
methods such as Lok Adalats, conciliation, arbitration, and mediation offer
several ways to settle disagreements short of official litigation.
Lok Adalats: These voluntary forums, which are governed by the Legal
Services Authorities Act of 1987, are where disagreements are resolved
peacefully via negotiation and conciliation. Their rulings are enforceable
and comparable to a civil court's judgment.
Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996: This Act established a strong
arbitration system in India and encouraged the settlement of business
disputes out of court. It was modeled after the UNCITRAL model. In order
to improve the efficiency of arbitration in India and streamline procedures,
the Act has undergone many amendments, most notably in 2015 and 2019.
Key Pillars of the System
· Independence: The judiciary is independent of the Executive
(Government) and the Legislature (Parliament) to ensure impartial
justice.
· Precedent: Decisions made by the Supreme Court are binding
on all other courts within India.
· Integrated Hierarchy: A case can travel from the lowest court to
the Supreme Court through the process of appeal
Lets not forget that the Indian legal system follows "Common Law"
principles, inherited from the British era, but heavily modified by the Indian
Constitution and subsequent statutes.

Part B: Powers and Jurisdiction of Supreme Court
History
The Supreme Court of India is the apex judicial body under the Constitution of
India. Article 124 of the Constitution states that “There shall be a Supreme
Court of India.” The Supreme Court came into existence on 26 January 1950
with the coming into force of the Constitution. The Supreme Court initially
functioned from the old Parliament House till it moved to the present building
located on Tilak Marg, New Delhi in 1958.
On 28 January 1950, two days after India became a Sovereign Democratic
Republic, the Supreme Court was inaugurated. The inauguration took place in
the Chamber of Princes in the old Parliament building where the Federal Court
of India sat for 12 years from 1937 to 1950.
The inaugural proceedings began at 9:45 am. It was attended by the first Chief
Justice of India, Harilal J. Kania and Judges of the Federal Court – Justices
Saiyid Fazl Ali, M. Patanjali Sastri, Mehr Chand Mahajan, Bijan Kumar
Mukherjea and S.R. Das, the Chief Justices of the High Courts of Allahabad,
Bombay, Madras, Orissa, Assam, Nagpur, Punjab, Saurashtra, Patiala and the
East Punjab States Union, Mysore, Hyderabad, Madhya Bharat and
Travancore-Cochin.
The proceedings were also graced by the Prime Minister of India,
ambassadors and diplomatic representatives of foreign States, the Attorney
General of India M.C. Setalvad, the Advocate Generals of Bombay, Madras,
Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, East Punjab, Orissa, Mysore, Hyderabad, and Madhya
Bharat and a large number of advocates. To ensure that the Rules of the
Supreme Court were published and the names of all the Advocates and agents
of the Federal Court were brought on the rolls of the Supreme Court, the
inaugural proceedings were put as part of the record of the Supreme Court.
After its inauguration on 28 January 1950, the Supreme Court commenced its
sittings in a part of the old Parliament House. The Court moved into a new
building in 1958. The first President of India Dr. Rajendra Prasad inaugurated
the present building of the Supreme Court of India on 4 August 1958. The
building is shaped to project the image of scales of justice. It has a 27.6 metre
high dome and a spacious colonnaded verandah. The Central Wing of the
building is the centre beam of the scales. The Chief Justice’s Court is the
largest of the courts located in the centre of the Central Wing. There is a life
size figure of Mahatma Gandhi, the apostle of truth and non-violence in the
courtyard opposite the Chief Justice’s Court. The statue was unveiled by the
26th Chief Justice of India, Justice A.M. Ahmadi on 1 August 1996. There is
also a 7-foot tall statue of Dr. B.R. Ambedkar which was unveiled by Hon’ble
President of India, Smt. Droupadi Murmu in the presence of the 50th Chief
Justice of India, Dr. Justice D.Y. Chandrachud on 26 November 2023. The
statue honours the architect of the Constitution and captures him in a lawyer’s
gown, holding a copy of the Constitution in his hand. To visit the building, one
may book a guided tour from the Supreme Court website or obtain the visitor’s
pass from the SuSwagatam portal or the front desk of the Supreme Court.
Three extensions were made to the original building- for the first time in 1979,
then in 1994, and again in 2015. In 1979, two New Wings – the East Wing and
the West Wing were added to the complex. There are 19 courtrooms in the
various wings of the building. In 1994, the second extension of the building was
made which connected the East and the West Wings.The third extension – the
New Extension Block near the Supreme Court Museum was inaugurated by
the then Chief Justice of India, Justice H.L. Dattu on 4 November 2015 and
some of the Sections from the existing building were shifted to the new
building. On 17 July 2019, Hon’ble President of India, Shri Ram Nath Kovind
inaugurated the Additional Building Complex of the Supreme Court of India.
The additional complex, with a total built up area of 1,80,700 sq. mts. has five
functional blocks and one service block. The curvature of the building rises
from the ground level in the colour scheme and sandstone external cladding,
drawing inspiration from the original architecture of the Supreme Court. The
Additional Building Complex also houses the New Judges’ Library.
The original Constitution of 1950 envisaged a Supreme Court with a Chief
Justice and 7 puisne Judges – leaving it to Parliament to increase this number.
In the early years, all the Judges of the Supreme Court sat together (en banc)
to hear the cases presented before them. Considering the increase in
workload, Parliament increased the number of Judges from 8 in 1950 to 11 in
1956, 14 in 1960, 18 in 1978, 26 in 1986, 31 in 2009 and 34 in 2019 (current
strength). Today, the Judges sit in Benches of two and three and come
together in larger Benches of 5 and more (Constitution Bench) to decide any
conflicting decisions between benches of the Supreme Court or any substantial
questions concerning the interpretation of the Constitution.
The proceedings of the Supreme Court are conducted in English. The practice
and procedure of working of the Registry on the judicial side is regulated by the
Supreme Court Rules, 2013 and Handbook on Practice and Procedure and
Office Procedure. The Supreme Court Officers and Servants (Conditions of
Service and Conduct) Rules, 1961 contains the rules with respect to the
conditions of service and conduct of the staff attached to the Supreme Court of
India.
CONSTITUTION OF SUPREME COURT
The Supreme Court of India comprises the Chief Justice of India and not more
than 33 other Judges appointed by the President of India. Supreme Court
Judges retire at the age of 65. In order to be appointed as a Judge of the
Supreme Court, a person must be a citizen of India and must have been, for at
least five years, a Judge of a High Court or an Advocate of a High Court for at
least 10 years or he must be, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished
jurist. The Constitution also contains provisions for a Judge of the High Court
who may be appointed as an ad-hoc Judge of the Supreme Court and for
retired Judges of the Supreme Court or High Courts to sit and act as Judges of
that Court. The independence of judiciary is part of the basic structure of the
Indian Constitution. A Supreme Court Judge cannot be removed from office
except by an order of the President passed after an address in each House of
Parliament supported by a majority of the total membership of that House and
by a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting, and
presented to the President in the same Session for such removal on the
ground of proved misbehavior or incapacity. A person who has been a Judge
of the Supreme Court is debarred from practicing in any court of law or before
any other authority in India.
SUPREME COURT REGISTRY
The administrative wing of the Supreme Court is known as the Registry. For
systematic functioning and efficient disposal of work, the Registry is divided
into two main wings, viz. Administration and Judicial, which are further divided
into various Divisions, Branches, Sections and Cells. The administrative power
for determining the work structure of the Court and the Registry exclusively
vests in the Chief Justice of India. The Secretary General who is of the rank of
Secretary to the Government of India is the highest Administrative Officer of
the Supreme Court. He is assisted by Registrars/Officers on Special Duty and
Additional Registrars who are assigned work of specific branches and they, in
turn, are assisted by Deputy Registrars, Assistant Registrars and other
officers/staff.
ATTORNEY GENERAL
The Attorney General of India is the highest law officer of the country
appointed by the President. He shall be a person qualified to be appointed as a
Judge of the Supreme Court. It is the duty of the Attorney General of India to
give advice to the Government of India upon such legal matters and to perform
such other duties of legal character as may be referred or assigned to him by
the President. In the performance of his duties, he has the right of audience in
all Courts in India as well as the right to take part in the proceedings of
Parliament without the right to vote. In discharge of his functions, the Attorney
General is assisted by a Solicitor General and Additional Solicitors Generals.
SUPREME COURT ADVOCATES
There are three categories of Advocates who are entitled to practise law
before the Supreme Court of India:-
SENIOR ADVOCATES
‘Senior Advocate’ means any advocate so designated under sub-section
(2) of Section 16 of the Advocates Act, 1961, and all such advocates
whose names were borne on the roll of the senior advocates of the Court
immediately before the commencement of Chapter III of the Advocates Act,
1961. All matters relating to the designation of Senior Advocates in the
Supreme Court of India are dealt with by a Permanent Committee known
as the “Committee for Designation of Senior Advocates” under the
Guidelines for Designation of Senior Advocates by the Supreme Court of
India, 2023.The Chief Justice of India or any Judge of the Supreme Court
may recommend the name of an advocate for being designated as a senior
advocate on being of the opinion that such an advocate who mainly
practises in the Supreme Court should be so designated by virtue of their
ability, standing at the Bar or special knowledge or experience in law. A
Senior Advocate is not entitled to appear in the Supreme Court without an
Advocate-on-Record. He is also not entitled to accept instructions to draw
pleadings or affidavits, advise on evidence or do any drafting work of an
analogous kind in any court or tribunal in India or undertake conveyancing
work of any kind whatsoever but this prohibition shall not extend to settling
any such matter as aforesaid in consultation with a junior.
ADVOCATES-ON-RECORD
Only these Advocates are entitled to file any matter or document before
the Supreme Court. They can also file an appearance or act for a party in
the Supreme Court.
OTHER ADVOCATES
These are the Advocates whose names are entered on the roll of any
State Bar Council maintained under the Advocates Act, 1961. They can
appear and argue any matter on behalf of a party in the Supreme Court but
they are not entitled to file any document or matter before the Court.
SUPREME COURT’S Major Jurisdictions
The Court's authority includes:
· Original Jurisdiction (Article 131): Exclusive, first-instance
handling of disputes between the Central Government and States,
or between States.
· Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32): Direct authority to enforce
fundamental rights via writs like habeas corpus and mandamus.
· Appellate Jurisdiction (Articles 132–134): Reviewing High Court
decisions in constitutional, civil, and criminal matters.
· Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143): Providing non-binding legal
opinions to the President on matters of public importance.
· Special Leave Petition (Article 136): Discretionary power to hear
appeals against any judgment from lower courts.
Significant Powers
· Judicial Review: Authority to invalidate legislative or executive
actions violating the Constitution.
· Court of Record (Article 129): Decisions act as legal precedents,
with power to punish for contempt.
· Complete Justice (Article 142): Power to issue orders necessary
for justice in pending cases.
· Review/Curative Petitions (Article 137): Revisiting own
judgments to correct errors.
Case Transfer (Article 139A): Authority to transfer cases between High Courts

HIGH COURT AND ITS POWERS
Welcome back learners! I am Amrita and today we shall be looking into the High Court and its
Powers.
Imagine a room where the stakes are high, the arguments are sharp, and the decisions shape not
just individual lives but sometimes entire policies. That is the High Court. It is not just a building
with judges in black robes; it is one of the most powerful constitutional institutions in India. For
many young citizens, the High Court may seem distant or complicated, but it actually plays a
direct role in protecting rights, checking government power, and ensuring justice across states.
In India’s judicial structure, the High Court stands just below the Supreme Court. Every state, or
group of states, has a High Court. For example, the High Court of Karnataka sits in Bengaluru,
the Bombay High Court serves Maharashtra and Goa, and the Calcutta High Court is one of the
oldest in the country. These courts are established under Part VI of the Constitution of India and
derive their authority directly from the Constitution, not from Parliament. This is important
because it means their existence and powers are constitutionally protected.
To understand the High Court, we need to first understand what kind of cases it hears. The High
Court has both original and appellate jurisdiction. Original jurisdiction means it can hear certain
cases directly, without them first going to a lower court. In some High Courts like the Bombay
High Court or the Calcutta High Court, certain civil matters of high value can be filed directly.
However, more commonly, High Courts act as appellate courts. This means they hear appeals
against decisions given by lower courts such as District Courts and Sessions Courts. If someone
feels that a trial court made an error in law or fact, they can approach the High Court for review.
One of the most powerful features of the High Court is its writ jurisdiction under Article 226 of
the Constitution. This power allows the High Court to issue writs for the enforcement of
fundamental rights and for any other purpose. This phrase “for any other purpose” is crucial
because it makes the High Court’s writ power even broader than that of the Supreme Court under
Article 32, which is limited to fundamental rights. Through writs like Habeas Corpus,
Mandamus, Certiorari, Prohibition, and Quo Warranto, High Courts can intervene when there is
injustice or abuse of authority.
Take the writ of Habeas Corpus, which literally means “produce the body.” If someone is
illegally detained, the High Court can order the authorities to produce the person before it and
justify the detention. During the Emergency period in India, the case of ADM Jabalpur v.
Shivkant Shukla went to the Supreme Court, but many High Courts had earlier taken a more
rights-friendly approach in habeas corpus petitions. This shows how High Courts can become
frontline defenders of liberty, especially in times of crisis.
Another significant power of the High Court is judicial review. Judicial review means the court
can examine laws and executive actions to see if they violate the Constitution. If a state law goes
against fundamental rights or exceeds the legislative competence of the state, the High Court can
strike it down. For example, in the case of Naz Foundation v. Government of NCT of Delhi, the
Delhi High Court read down Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code to decriminalise consensual
same-sex relations between adults. This 2009 judgment was a landmark moment for LGBTQ+
rights in India. Although the Supreme Court later reversed it and then finally restored the
position in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, the Delhi High Court’s decision was a bold
example of how a High Court can push constitutional morality forward.
High Courts also supervise the functioning of lower courts within their territorial jurisdiction.
Under Article 227, they have the power of superintendence over all courts and tribunals in their
state. This ensures consistency and accountability in the judicial system. If a lower court acts
beyond its jurisdiction or makes a serious procedural error, the High Court can correct it. This
supervisory role is essential to maintaining the integrity of the justice system.
Another important power is contempt jurisdiction. If someone disobeys a High Court’s order or
behaves in a way that undermines the authority of the court, the High Court can initiate contempt
proceedings. This is not about ego or prestige; it is about ensuring that court orders are respected
and implemented. Without this power, judicial decisions could be ignored without consequence.
High Courts also play a vital role in public interest litigation, commonly known as PIL. PIL
allows individuals or groups to approach the court not just for personal grievances but for
broader public causes. For example, the Uttarakhand High Court in 2017 declared the rivers
Ganga and Yamuna as legal persons in the case of Mohd. Salim v. State of Uttarakhand.
Although the Supreme Court later stayed this decision, it reflected how High Courts can
experiment with innovative legal ideas to address environmental concerns.
In the Bombay High Court, several environmental cases have led to significant policy changes in
Mumbai, including rulings on coastal regulation zones and illegal constructions. In the Madras
High Court, strong observations have often been made regarding educational reforms and state
accountability. These examples show that High Courts are not passive institutions; they actively
shape governance and policy through constitutional interpretation.
Another landmark decision from a High Court was in the case of Justice K.S. Puttaswamy
(Retd.) v. Union of India at the Karnataka High Court stage. Although the final ruling on the
right to privacy came from the Supreme Court, the initial petition challenging Aadhaar began in
the Karnataka High Court. This highlights how High Courts often become the starting point for
national constitutional debates.
High Courts also handle criminal appeals in serious cases. If a Sessions Court awards a death
sentence, it must be confirmed by the High Court before it can be carried out. This mandatory
confirmation ensures an additional layer of scrutiny in cases involving the most severe
punishment. The High Court can confirm, commute, or overturn such sentences. This
demonstrates how deeply the High Court is involved in safeguarding life and liberty.
The judges of a High Court are appointed by the President of India in consultation with the Chief
Justice of India and the Governor of the concerned state. Their independence is protected by
security of tenure and fixed service conditions. This independence is crucial because High Courts
often hear cases against state governments. If judges were easily removable or dependent on the
executive, their ability to deliver fair judgments would be compromised.
For young citizens, the High Court is not just a distant constitutional body. It is a place where
students have challenged arbitrary university regulations, where activists have questioned
internet shutdowns, and where ordinary citizens have sought protection against illegal land
acquisition. During various internet shutdowns in different states, High Courts have examined
whether such actions were proportionate and lawful, reinforcing the idea that digital rights are
also constitutional rights.
What makes the High Court especially powerful is its accessibility compared to the Supreme
Court. While the Supreme Court sits only in New Delhi, High Courts are located within states,
making them more reachable for ordinary people. This proximity makes them crucial for
decentralised justice delivery.
In recent years, High Courts have also adapted to technology. During the COVID-19 pandemic,
many High Courts conducted virtual hearings to ensure that access to justice did not stop. This
flexibility demonstrated that even traditional institutions can evolve with changing times.
At its core, the High Court is a constitutional guardian. It balances three major functions:
resolving disputes, protecting rights, and checking government power. Through writs, judicial
review, appellate jurisdiction, and supervisory powers, it ensures that the rule of law is not just a
textbook concept but a lived reality.
For a young audience, it is important to see the High Court not as an intimidating authority but as
a space where the Constitution comes alive. Every time a High Court questions a discriminatory
rule, halts an unlawful arrest, or directs the government to act fairly, it reinforces the promise of
justice, liberty, equality, and dignity.
Understanding the High Court means understanding that democracy is not only about elections
and political debates. It is also about institutions that quietly and consistently protect the
framework within which politics operates. The High Court is one such institution. It stands as a
bridge between the Constitution and the everyday lives of people, ensuring that power is
exercised responsibly and rights are respected.
So the next time you hear about a High Court judgment in the news, remember that it is not just
legal jargon. It is part of a larger story about how constitutional values are defended, contested,
and strengthened in India’s democracy.

Welcome to this module on Subordinate Courts in India.
I am Sneha Yadav.
In today’s session, we will explore the structure, functioning, challenges, and
significance of subordinate courts within India’s judicial system. While much public
attention is given to constitutional courts, it is at the district and local levels where the
majority of justice is actually delivered. Understanding subordinate courts is essential
not only for students of political science and law, but also for citizens who wish to
understand how the rule of law functions in everyday life.
Let us begin with a simple but important question:
When a property dispute arises between family members, where is the case filed?
When a criminal offence such as theft or assault occurs, which court conducts the trial?
When a woman seeks maintenance or a worker seeks compensation, where do they
go?
In most instances, the answer is the subordinate courts.
When we think of the Indian judiciary, we often imagine the authority of the Supreme
Court of India or the constitutional prominence of the High Courts of India. These courts
interpret the Constitution, decide landmark cases, and shape national legal discourse.
However, they handle only a small fraction of the total cases filed in India. The vast
majority of cases — civil and criminal — are dealt with by subordinate courts functioning
at the district and sub-district levels.
Subordinate courts derive their constitutional foundation from Articles 233 to 237 of the
Constitution of India. These provisions regulate the appointment, recruitment, and
control of judicial officers at the district level. District Judges are appointed by the
Governor in consultation with the High Court. Importantly, Article 235 vests
administrative control over subordinate courts in the High Courts. This constitutional
arrangement is designed to safeguard judicial independence at the grassroots level by
limiting executive interference.
Structurally, subordinate courts are divided into civil and criminal branches.
On the civil side, the hierarchy generally includes the Civil Judge (Junior Division), Civil
Judge (Senior Division), Additional District Judge, and District Judge. Civil courts
adjudicate disputes involving private rights — such as property disagreements,
contractual conflicts, inheritance claims, and family matters. For example, if two siblings
contest ownership of ancestral land, the dispute would typically begin before a Civil
Judge. If one party is dissatisfied with the judgment, an appeal can be filed before the
District Court.
On the criminal side, the structure includes Judicial Magistrates of various classes, the
Chief Judicial Magistrate, and the Sessions Court. Magistrates try less serious offences,
while serious crimes such as murder, rape, or large-scale financial fraud are tried by the
Sessions Court. The Sessions Court has the authority to impose severe punishments,
including life imprisonment and, subject to confirmation by the High Court, the death
penalty.
Interestingly, the District Judge and the Sessions Judge are often the same individual,
exercising civil and criminal jurisdiction separately. This integrated structure allows for
administrative efficiency while maintaining functional specialization.
The importance of subordinate courts becomes clearer when we examine the volume of
cases they handle. Nearly eighty-five to ninety percent of all pending cases in India are
concentrated at the district and subordinate levels. This means that the real experience
of justice for most citizens is shaped by how effectively these courts function.
Consider a practical example. A daily wage worker accused of a minor offence may
seek bail before a magistrate. If the court is overburdened and hearings are repeatedly
delayed, that individual may remain in custody longer than necessary. Similarly, a
woman seeking maintenance from her spouse may depend on timely judicial
intervention for financial survival. Delays in subordinate courts directly impact
fundamental rights, particularly the right to life and personal liberty under Article 21 of
the Constitution.
Thus, inefficiencies at the district level are not merely procedural concerns — they raise
constitutional and democratic questions.
Over time, the subordinate judiciary has evolved to address emerging societal needs.
Specialized courts have been established within the district framework. Family Courts
aim to resolve matrimonial disputes in a less adversarial manner. Motor Accident Claims
Tribunals determine compensation for road accident victims. Commercial Courts
expedite high-value business disputes to promote economic efficiency. Special courts
under laws such as the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act ensure focused
and sensitive handling of crimes against minors.
For instance, in a case involving child abuse, a designated Special Court ensures that
proceedings are conducted with sensitivity and urgency. This specialization reflects an
attempt to balance efficiency with justice.
Despite their critical role, subordinate courts face substantial challenges. Judicial
vacancies remain a persistent issue across states. Many courts operate with inadequate
infrastructure — limited courtrooms, insufficient staff, and delayed digitization.
Procedural complexities and frequent adjournments contribute to significant case
backlogs.
The issue of undertrial detention is particularly concerning. A large proportion of India’s
prison population consists of undertrial prisoners whose cases are pending before
subordinate criminal courts. This highlights systemic strain and raises concerns about
access to timely justice.
Recognizing these challenges, several reform initiatives have been introduced. The
e-Courts Mission Mode Project aims to digitize records, enable online filings, and
improve transparency. The National Judicial Data Grid provides real-time data on case
pendency and disposal rates. Fast-track courts have been established to expedite
sensitive or long-pending cases. Alternative Dispute Resolution mechanisms, including
mediation and Lok Adalats, are increasingly encouraged to reduce formal litigation
burden.
However, technological reform alone cannot resolve structural issues. Strengthening
subordinate courts requires timely recruitment, capacity building, improved
infrastructure, and better case management systems.
From a political science perspective, subordinate courts play a crucial role in sustaining
democratic legitimacy. Citizens evaluate the effectiveness of governance based on their
lived experiences. If disputes are resolved fairly and efficiently, public trust in institutions
strengthens. If justice is delayed or inaccessible, faith in the rule of law diminishes.
Subordinate courts are therefore not merely administrative units — they are central to
the functioning of democracy.
In conclusion, while constitutional courts define legal principles, subordinate courts
translate those principles into everyday realities. They decide matters that directly affect
citizens’ lives — property, liberty, compensation, accountability. Their functioning
determines whether the promise of justice is meaningful or merely aspirational.
Understanding subordinate courts allows us to appreciate the operational depth of
India’s judiciary. They represent the point where constitutional ideals meet social
realities.
Thank you for joining this session on Subordinate Courts in India. Until then, remember:
the strength of a democracy is measured not only by its apex institutions, but by how
effectively justice is delivered at the grassroots.

Welcome back learners,
Today, instead of only talking about classic cases like Kesavananda Bharati or Maneka

that show the Indian judiciary in action right now. These cases help us see how the
Constitution is interpreted in today’s context, how courts respond to issues of
representation, individual rights, and government power, and why following current
judgments is essential to understanding living constitutionalism. [Pause]
First, remember that Parliament makes laws, and courts interpret and test those laws
against the Constitution. When new controversies arise around reservations, elections,
personal liberty, or federal power, it is often the Supreme Court and High Courts that
clarify what the law really means, strike down provisions that violate fundamental rights,
or direct governments to change procedures or policies. So, if you want to understand
how the Indian Constitution works in 2026, you cannot stop at old case law; you have to
look at recent judicial developments as well. [Pause]
Let us begin with one of the most talked-about recent cases: the challenge to the
Electoral Bond Scheme in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India,
2024. For several years, electoral bonds allowed individuals and companies to donate
large sums of money to political parties while keeping their identity secret from the
public. Critics argued that this created opaque political funding, gave citizens no way to
know who was funding whom, and violated the right to information about matters
affecting public life. In a major decision delivered in 2024, the Supreme Court held key
aspects of the scheme unconstitutional. The Court reasoned that transparency in
political funding is closely linked to free and fair elections, that voters have a right to
know who finances the parties they vote for, and that secrecy on such a massive scale
can distort the democratic process. As a result, the existing electoral bonds were
invalidated, and the judgment pushed the system back toward more accountable
political finance. For students of the Constitution, this is a concrete example of the Court
protecting the democratic structure by insisting on openness in the political market.
[Pause]
Let us move on to the next case concerning Delhi’s governance and the power tussle
between the elected Delhi government and the Lieutenant Governor. In Government of
NCT of Delhi v. Union of India, 2023, the Supreme Court examined how much
executive power the elected government of Delhi has, in what matters the LG can
overrule or act independently, and how the Constitution’s special provisions for Union
Territories apply to the National Capital Territory. One key issue was who controls
“services” in Delhi, meaning the civil servants. The Court held that in a federal
democracy, the elected government must have substantial control over the
administrative machinery that implements its decisions, because excessive interference
by the LG would undermine the mandate given by the people to the Delhi Assembly.
While Parliament later responded with legislation altering this balance, the judgments
remain crucial because they illustrate how the judiciary defends the spirit of
representative government and tries to balance national-level concerns with local
democratic accountability. [Pause]
Now let us turn to bail, “jail is the exception,” and personal liberty. Post-2023, several
Supreme Court decisions have strongly emphasized that bail, not jail, is the rule,
particularly in cases under stringent laws like the UAPA, or the Unlawful Activities
Prevention Act. In one widely discussed judgment, the Court noted that personal liberty
under Article 21 cannot be casually curtailed by keeping people in prolonged pre-trial
detention. Even under special laws, trial courts and High Courts must carefully examine
the evidence and the necessity of continued custody. The idea that “bail is the rule, jail
is the exception” is part of the constitutional protection of liberty. There were also cases
where the Court criticized inordinate delays in trials and directed that if the state takes
too long to conduct a trial, continued imprisonment becomes unreasonable. Speedy trial
is not just a policy preference; it is a constitutional right embedded in Article 21. For
students, these judgments are essential because they turn Article 21 into a practical
shield for ordinary people caught in criminal cases and show the judiciary actively
correcting the culture of long custody. [Pause]
Let us now move to affirmative action and reservation policies. Recent years have seen
important decisions around reservation and social justice, especially after the Janhit
Abhiyan v. Union of India, 2022 judgment, which upheld the 103rd Constitutional
Amendment introducing EWS reservation. Later cases and legal commentary have
continued to explore how reservations should be balanced with merit, whether and how
far economic criteria can be separated from caste-based disadvantage, and how new
categories affect existing SEBC communities. For example, subsequent cases and legal
analysis have questioned how states implement horizontal and vertical reservations and
examined cut-off marks and benchmark disability reservations, ensuring that
marginalized groups are not denied seats through technicalities. The broader takeaway
is that the judiciary is constantly revisiting how social justice is operationalized, and
even after Parliament creates new reservation categories, courts test their actual impact
on marginalized communities and on equality under Article 14. [Pause]
Now let us consider the recent judgments dealing with the Scheduled Castes and
Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act. In these cases, the Supreme Court
clarified that mere use of abusive language is not automatically an atrocity. For an
offense under the Act, the insult must have a clear link to the person’s caste or tribe
identity and occur in public view or within the specific legal framework. At the same
time, the Court emphasized that the purpose of the Act is to protect marginalized
communities from humiliation and violence rooted in caste discrimination. Courts must
therefore interpret the law in a way that does not trivialize the lived experiences of
discrimination, but also avoids misuse. For students of the Constitution, these cases
illustrate how statutory interpretation and human rights are balanced, while the judiciary
tries to protect SC/ST communities robustly and keep the criminal justice system fair
and focused on genuine atrocities. [Pause]
Let us move on to digital rights, expression, and new technologies. Recent judgments
have also touched on digital spaces and expression, though this remains an evolving
area. In cases involving social media platforms or digital content, the Supreme Court
and High Courts have had to consider how existing laws on defamation, obscenity, and
public order apply in online environments, and whether government actions restricting
apps or content meet constitutional tests of reasonableness and proportionality. These
cases show the judiciary grappling with the tension between regulation and free speech,
as well as the need to protect citizens’ rights without ignoring threats like
misinformation, hate speech, or security concerns. This area is important because it
demonstrates how judges extend constitutional doctrines to new technologies and
reminds us that rights like Article 19(1)(a), freedom of speech, are tested and
reinterpreted in the context of WhatsApp, Instagram, and other platforms used daily.
[Pause]
Let us now turn to service delivery, local bodies, and elections. Although not as famous
as national-level political cases, there have been recent judgments focusing on local
body elections and the constitutional mandate of regular polls. The Supreme Court has
reiterated that State Election Commissions cannot delay local body elections indefinitely
on vague grounds. Local governments must be renewed through elections at regular
intervals, as envisaged in the 73rd and 74th Amendments. In one observation, the Court
stressed that democracy at the grassroots is not optional, and that states have a
constitutional obligation to conduct Panchayat and municipal elections instead of
running them with nominated or ad hoc bodies. This is directly relevant to local
governance because it shows the judiciary stepping in when states try to postpone or
avoid elections, and it connects constitutional provisions on local bodies with real
enforcement from the Court. [Pause]
To close, remember this: the judiciary is not just a historical institution that gave us
famous old cases. It is a living actor, responding to new laws, new technologies, and
new conflicts every year. By looking at post-2023 judgments, you see how courts
protect the ideals of the Constitution in the present, how Parliament, state governments,
and local bodies are continuously checked against constitutional values, and how
citizens and civil society use litigation to challenge policies from electoral bonds to
delayed elections and prolonged pre-trial custody.

Good morning everyone.
Today, we are going to discuss one of the most fascinating aspects of the Indian
Constitution—the role of the judiciary in protecting constitutional values. The Supreme Court of
India and the High Courts are often described as the guardians of the Constitution because they
ensure that every law and executive action conforms to constitutional principles. Today, we will
examine a different set of judgments—cases that have transformed everyday life in India. These
judgments illustrate how constitutional values have evolved over time and how the judiciary has
responded to changing societal needs.
As we proceed, I encourage you to think beyond legal principles. Ask yourselves: How did
these decisions affect ordinary citizens? How did they change public policy? Why are they
still relevant today?
Let us begin our journey with a case that confronted one of the most fundamental constitutional
questions: Can the State take away the life of one of its citizens? The "Rarest of Rare"
Doctrine, right? Imagine that a person has been convicted of committing a brutal murder. Society
demands justice, the victims' families seek closure, and the law permits capital punishment. But
an important constitutional question arises: Should the State have the power to execute a
person? This question reached the Supreme Court in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab in 1980.
Bachan Singh had been convicted of murder and sentenced to death. Before the Supreme Court,
his lawyers challenged the constitutional validity of the death penalty itself. They argued that
capital punishment violated Article 21, which guarantees that no person shall be deprived of life
or personal liberty except according to the procedure established by law. The petitioners
contended that the death penalty was cruel, irreversible, and incompatible with the constitutional
commitment to human dignity. They argued that the right to life is the most precious of all
Fundamental Rights and that taking away life through judicial execution should not be
constitutionally permissible. On the other hand, the State argued that Parliament had consciously
retained the death penalty in the Indian Penal Code. It maintained that certain crimes are so
heinous that the interests of society demand the highest possible punishment.
The Supreme Court faced a difficult constitutional dilemma. It had to balance two competing
concerns: the protection of individual rights and the responsibility of the State to punish the
gravest crimes. The Court ultimately upheld the constitutional validity of the death penalty.
However, it imposed a very significant limitation. The judges held that the death sentence should
be awarded only in the "rarest of rare" cases, where life imprisonment would be wholly
inadequate.
What exactly does "rarest of rare" mean?
The Court explained that judges must consider both the nature of the crime and the
circumstances of the offender. Factors such as brutality, premeditation, the possibility of reform,
the age of the accused, and mitigating circumstances must all be carefully evaluated before
imposing the death penalty. This doctrine fundamentally changed India's approach to capital
punishment. The judgment did not abolish the death penalty, but it ensured that it would remain
an exceptional punishment rather than the ordinary rule. Even today, whenever Indian courts
consider imposing the death penalty, they apply the principles established in Bachan Singh. This
judgment demonstrates an important constitutional principle. The Constitution permits the State
to punish crime, but it also insists that punishment must be proportionate, fair, and consistent
with human dignity.
Think about this question: If there is even the slightest possibility that an innocent person may
be convicted, should the death penalty continue to exist? Different countries have answered this
question differently, and the debate continues across the world.
While Bachan Singh dealt with punishment after conviction, another important constitutional
concern remained unresolved. What happens before conviction? What safeguards exist when an
ordinary citizen is arrested by the police? Could the police detain someone illegally, torture them,
or even cause their death while in custody? These questions became increasingly urgent during
the 1980s and 1990s, when reports of custodial violence and custodial deaths became alarmingly
frequent.
Imagine that one day the police arrive at someone's house and arrest them. Their family members
are not informed where they have been taken. They disappear into police custody for several
days. Later, they are found seriously injured—or worse, dead. Unfortunately, such incidents were
not uncommon in India during the late twentieth century. Concerned by increasing reports of
custodial deaths, D.K. Basu, who was the Executive Chairman of the Legal Aid Services of West
Bengal, wrote a letter to the Chief Justice of India highlighting the growing problem. The
Supreme Court treated this letter as a Public Interest Litigation (PIL), demonstrating that
constitutional justice could begin with something as simple as a citizen drawing attention to a
systemic problem. The Court observed that custodial torture strikes at the very heart of the
Constitution. Articles 21 and 22 guarantee every individual the right to life, personal liberty, and
protection against arbitrary arrest. These protections do not disappear simply because a person
has been accused of committing a crime.
The Supreme Court recognised that torture is not merely a violation of criminal law; it is also a
violation of constitutional rights. To prevent abuse, the Court laid down eleven mandatory
guidelines that every police officer must follow during arrest and detention. Some of the most
important guidelines included:
● Police officers must wear clear identification badges displaying their names and ranks.
● An arrest memo must be prepared and signed by both the arrested person and a witness.
● A friend or family member of the arrested person must be informed immediately.
● The arrested individual has the right to consult a lawyer.
● Medical examinations must be conducted regularly during detention.
● Detailed records of the arrest must be maintained.
● Information regarding the arrest should be communicated to the relevant authorities
without delay.
These directions transformed policing in India. Today, many of these safeguards have been
incorporated into statutory law and police procedures. Whenever we hear about arrest procedures
on television or read about them in newspapers, many of those requirements originate from the
D.K. Basu judgment. Perhaps the greatest contribution of this case lies in its recognition that
constitutional rights belong to everyone—even those accused of crimes. The Constitution
does not distinguish between "good citizens" and "bad citizens." Every person possesses dignity,
and that dignity must be respected by the State.
Let me ask you a question. If police officers violate these constitutional safeguards while
investigating a serious crime, should the evidence they collect still be accepted by the courts?
This question continues to challenge legal systems across the world. The judiciary had
strengthened protections against abuse by law enforcement. However, constitutional rights are
not threatened only in police stations. Sometimes discrimination occurs in workplaces,
educational institutions, and other everyday environments.
One incident involving a rural social worker would eventually lead to one of the most influential
judgments on women's rights in India. The story of Vishaka begins not inside a courtroom but in
a small village in Rajasthan. A woman named Bhanwari Devi worked as a social worker under a
government programme aimed at preventing child marriage. While performing her official
duties, she attempted to stop the marriage of a young girl. Her actions angered influential
members of the local community. In retaliation, Bhanwari Devi was brutally gang-raped. The
criminal justice system failed her. The accused were acquitted by the trial court, and there was no
specific law in India dealing with sexual harassment at the workplace or ensuring a safe working
environment for women. Recognising this legal vacuum, several women's rights organisations
came together under the name Vishaka and filed a Public Interest Litigation before the Supreme
Court. The constitutional question before the Court was profound:
Can women truly enjoy equality and the freedom to work if they are constantly threatened
by sexual harassment? The Supreme Court answered with a clear and unequivocal no.
The Court held that sexual harassment violates multiple Fundamental Rights, including:
● Article 14 — Equality before the law.
● Article 15 — Protection against discrimination.
● Article 19(1)(g) — Freedom to practise any profession or occupation.
● Article 21 — Right to life with dignity.
One remarkable feature of this judgment was the Court's reliance on international law. India had
ratified the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women
(CEDAW). Since Parliament had not enacted a domestic law on workplace sexual harassment,
the Supreme Court used India's international obligations to frame legally binding guidelines.
These became known as the Vishaka Guidelines. For more than fifteen years, these judicial
guidelines served as the law of the land until Parliament enacted the Sexual Harassment of
Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.
The Vishaka judgment demonstrated how the judiciary stepped in when the legislature had not
yet enacted a law to protect women at the workplace. By the beginning of the twenty-first
century, however, Indian society was undergoing another transformation. The rapid spread of the
internet, smartphones, and social media fundamentally changed how people communicated,
expressed opinions, and participated in democratic life. These developments presented new
constitutional questions. Could the government regulate online speech in the same manner as
traditional speech? What limits could be placed on digital expression? The Supreme Court
addressed these questions in one of the most significant free speech cases of the digital era.
Imagine posting a critical comment about a politician on social media. A few hours later, the
police arrive at your doorstep and arrest you because someone claims that your post is
"offensive" or "annoying." This may sound extreme, but before 2015, such incidents were
occurring across India. The reason was Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000.
The provision criminalised sending information through electronic communication that was
considered "grossly offensive," "menacing," or caused "annoyance," "inconvenience," or
"insult." The problem was that these terms were not clearly defined. What one person considered
offensive, another might view as legitimate criticism. Several arrests highlighted the misuse of
the provision. One of the most widely reported incidents involved two young women from
Maharashtra. One of them questioned on Facebook why Mumbai had virtually shut down
following the death of a political leader, while the other merely "liked" the post. Both were
arrested under Section 66A. Similar arrests were made for cartoons, memes, and comments on
social media. Concerned about the chilling effect on free speech, Shreya Singhal, a law student,
filed a petition before the Supreme Court challenging the constitutional validity of Section 66A.
The principal constitutional question before the Court was whether Section 66A violated Article
19(1)(a), which guarantees the freedom of speech and expression. The Government argued that
the provision was necessary to maintain public order and prevent the misuse of the internet. It
maintained that online communication spreads rapidly and can potentially incite violence or
spread misinformation. The petitioners, however, argued that the provision was vague,
overbroad, and arbitrary. Citizens could not reasonably know what constituted an offence
because terms such as "annoyance" or "inconvenience" had no precise legal meaning. This
uncertainty encouraged arbitrary arrests and discouraged people from expressing their views.
The Supreme Court agreed with the petitioners. The Court held that the Constitution protects not
only popular opinions but also criticism, satire, dissent, and even speech that may be unpopular
or uncomfortable. Freedom of expression cannot exist if people constantly fear criminal
prosecution for expressing their views.
The Court also clarified an important constitutional distinction between discussion, advocacy,
and incitement.
It observed that:
● Discussion is protected.
● Advocacy of ideas is also protected.
● Only speech that amounts to incitement to violence or public disorder may legitimately
be restricted under Article 19(2).
Since Section 66A criminalised speech merely because it caused annoyance or inconvenience, it
exceeded the constitutional limits placed on restrictions under Article 19(2). Consequently, the
Supreme Court struck down Section 66A in its entirety. This judgment remains one of the
strongest affirmations of freedom of speech in independent India. It also established that
constitutional rights remain fully applicable in the digital world.
As India embraced digital freedoms, the judiciary was also called upon to ensure that
constitutional rights were meaningful for every individual, including those who had long
experienced discrimination and exclusion. One such case involved a woman whose treatment
during a routine flight raised important questions about dignity and equality. In February 2012,
Jeeja Ghosh, an accomplished disability rights activist with cerebral palsy, boarded a SpiceJet
flight from Kolkata to Goa. She had a valid ticket and was travelling independently. However,
shortly before take-off, airline officials asked her to leave the aircraft. They assumed that
because of her disability she was unfit to travel alone. She was removed from the flight despite
explaining that she had travelled independently on numerous occasions. The incident was deeply
humiliating.
Jeeja Ghosh approached the Supreme Court, arguing that her removal from the aircraft violated
her dignity and equality as guaranteed by the Constitution. The constitutional question before the
Court was not simply whether an airline had acted improperly. It was much broader:
Can a person's disability become the basis for denying them equal treatment and
participation in public life? The Supreme Court answered firmly in the negative. The Court
observed that disability should never be viewed as incapacity. Rather, society must remove
barriers that prevent persons with disabilities from participating equally. The judges held that
dignity lies at the heart of the Constitution. Every individual deserves to be treated with respect
regardless of physical or mental ability. The Court directed SpiceJet to pay compensation to Jeeja
Ghosh and criticised the insensitive behaviour of the airline staff. Beyond compensation,
however, the judgment carried a much broader message.
It encouraged institutions to move away from charity-based approaches towards disability and
instead adopt a rights-based approach. Persons with disabilities are not objects of sympathy;
they are rights-bearing citizens entitled to equal opportunities. Today, the principles laid down in
Jeeja Ghosh continue to influence discussions on accessibility, inclusive education, public
transportation, and workplace inclusion. While the judiciary was expanding the meaning of
equality for persons with disabilities, another important constitutional debate was unfolding.
Could personal laws, followed by religious communities, be tested against constitutional
principles of equality and dignity? This question reached the Supreme Court in Shayara Bano v.
Union of India. Shayara Bano had been married for several years when her husband ended the
marriage by pronouncing "talaq" three times—a practice commonly known as instant triple
talaq or talaq-e-biddat. Under this practice, a Muslim husband could unilaterally and
instantaneously dissolve the marriage without any scope for reconciliation or legal process.
Feeling that the practice violated her dignity and equality, Shayara Bano approached the
Supreme Court. The case generated nationwide debate. Some argued that triple talaq formed part
of religious freedom protected under Article 25 of the Constitution. Others argued that no
religious practice could survive constitutional scrutiny if it violated equality and justice. A
Constitution Bench of five judges heard the matter.
Although the judges differed in some aspects of their reasoning, the majority concluded that
instant triple talaq was unconstitutional and legally invalid.
The Court observed that a practice allowing one spouse to unilaterally terminate a marriage
without procedural safeguards was manifestly arbitrary and incompatible with constitutional
values. The judgment was significant because it reflected the principle of constitutional
morality—the idea that constitutional values such as equality, dignity, and justice must guide the
interpretation of laws and practices. The decision was welcomed by many Muslim women's
organisations, while others viewed it as judicial interference in matters of personal law.
Following the judgment, Parliament enacted the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on
Marriage) Act, 2019, making the pronouncement of instant triple talaq a punishable offence.
Whether one agrees or disagrees with the legislation, the case illustrates how judicial decisions
often initiate broader legal and political reforms. The year 2017 witnessed another judgment that
had a profound impact on Indian society. Unlike most constitutional cases, this one was preceded
by nationwide protests, candlelight marches, and an unprecedented public demand for justice.
The case became known simply as the Nirbhaya Case. On the night of 16 December 2012, a
23-year-old physiotherapy student and her friend boarded a private bus in Delhi. What followed
was one of the most horrific crimes in modern Indian history. The young woman was brutally
assaulted and raped by six men. She sustained severe injuries and, despite receiving treatment in
India and later in Singapore, succumbed to her injuries. The incident shocked the nation.
Millions of people across India protested against violence against women, demanding stronger
laws, faster investigations, and greater accountability. The accused were prosecuted before a
fast-track court. Four adult accused were eventually convicted and sentenced to death. Their
convictions were later upheld by the Delhi High Court and finally by the Supreme Court in
Mukesh & Another v. State (NCT of Delhi). The Supreme Court described the crime as one of
exceptional brutality and upheld the death sentences, concluding that it fell within the "rarest of
rare" category established in Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab.
Notice how constitutional jurisprudence develops over time. The doctrine formulated in 1980
was applied nearly four decades later in one of the country's most significant criminal cases. The
constitutional importance of the Nirbhaya case extends beyond the convictions. The case
prompted the establishment of the Justice J.S. Verma Committee, which recommended
comprehensive reforms to India's criminal law relating to sexual offences. Many of these
recommendations were incorporated into the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act, 2013. The
legislation expanded the definition of sexual offences, introduced stricter punishments,
strengthened victim protections, and recognised offences such as stalking and voyeurism. The
Nirbhaya case demonstrates how judicial decisions, public opinion, legislative action, and
constitutional values can work together to reshape the legal landscape.
Our final two cases continue this journey by examining how the judiciary responded to changing
social norms and technological developments. One challenged outdated notions of marriage and
gender equality, while the other addressed one of the defining constitutional questions of the
digital era—whether access to the internet is essential for the exercise of fundamental rights.
Let us begin with a simple question. Should adultery be a criminal offence?
Most of you might respond that adultery is morally wrong or that it may be a ground for divorce.
But should it lead to imprisonment? Until 2018, the answer under Indian law was yes. Section
497 of the Indian Penal Code made adultery a criminal offence. However, what made the law
particularly controversial was the way it was drafted. Under the provision, only a man who had a
sexual relationship with a married woman without her husband's consent could be punished. The
married woman herself could never be prosecuted. Even more strikingly, the offence was treated
as one committed against the husband rather than against the marriage itself. This meant that the
law viewed a married woman almost as the property of her husband. If the husband consented to
the relationship, no offence was committed. If he did not, the other man could be prosecuted.
Notice the assumptions behind this law. It did not recognise women as equal partners capable of
making independent choices. Instead, it reflected nineteenth-century patriarchal ideas that were
increasingly incompatible with constitutional values. Joseph Shine, an Indian citizen residing
abroad, challenged the constitutional validity of Section 497 before the Supreme Court.
The principal constitutional question was:
Can the State criminalise adultery in a manner that discriminates on the basis of gender
and undermines individual autonomy? The Union Government argued that the law protected
the institution of marriage and promoted family stability. The petitioners, however, argued that
the provision violated Articles 14, 15, and 21 by denying women equality, treating them
unequally before the law, and interfering with personal autonomy. A Constitution Bench of five
judges unanimously struck down Section 497. The Court held that marriage does not extinguish
the constitutional rights of either spouse. One of the most significant observations made by the
Court was that a woman is not the property of her husband. The judges further explained that
while adultery may be morally unacceptable and may constitute a valid ground for divorce under
civil law, it should not be treated as a criminal offence punishable by imprisonment. The
judgment introduced students to an important constitutional concept—constitutional morality.
Constitutional morality requires that laws be judged not merely on the basis of tradition or social
acceptance but against the values embodied in the Constitution itself—liberty, equality, dignity,
and individual autonomy. The Court observed that constitutional rights cannot be sacrificed
simply because a law reflects outdated social norms. The Joseph Shine judgment therefore
represents a broader shift in constitutional thinking. It recognises marriage as a relationship
between equals rather than one based on hierarchy and control. At the same time, the Court
clarified that the judgment did not legalise adultery in the moral sense. Adultery continues to be
a ground for divorce and may have civil consequences, but it is no longer a criminal offence.
This distinction is important. The Constitution does not require the State to criminalise every act
that society considers immoral. Criminal law should be used only where it is necessary to protect
public interests and constitutional values. Think about this question: Should criminal law
regulate private relationships between consenting adults, or should such matters remain within
the domain of personal morality and family law? The Joseph Shine judgment encourages us to
reflect on this distinction.
While Joseph Shine addressed equality within personal relationships, another constitutional
challenge arose from an entirely different context. By 2020, the internet had become central to
modern life. Students attended online classes, businesses conducted transactions digitally,
journalists published news online, and citizens used social media to express opinions and
participate in democratic debates. But what happens when the government suspends internet
services? Can access to the internet be restricted indefinitely?
These questions came before the Supreme Court in Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India.
To understand this case, we must briefly revisit the events of August 2019. On 5 August 2019,
Parliament abrogated the special constitutional status of the former State of Jammu and Kashmir
under Article 370. Anticipating unrest, the Government imposed extensive security measures.
These included restrictions on movement, preventive detention of political leaders, and a
complete shutdown of internet and communication services across the region. The restrictions
continued for several months. For ordinary citizens, the consequences were enormous. Students
could not access educational resources. Doctors found it difficult to communicate with hospitals.
Businesses relying on online transactions suffered significant losses. Journalists faced immense
challenges in gathering and publishing news. Among those affected was Anuradha Bhasin, the
Executive Editor of the Kashmir Times. She approached the Supreme Court, arguing that the
communication restrictions severely affected press freedom and citizens' constitutional rights.
The Supreme Court had to consider several important constitutional questions.
Does freedom of speech extend to the internet?
Can the government suspend internet services indefinitely?
What limits should exist on executive power during situations involving national security?
The Government argued that the restrictions were necessary to maintain public order and protect
national security. The petitioners accepted that restrictions may sometimes be justified but argued
that such restrictions must remain temporary, proportionate, and subject to judicial review. The
Supreme Court recognised that although the Constitution does not expressly mention the
internet, modern constitutional rights are often exercised through digital platforms. Today, people
express opinions, conduct business, pursue education, access healthcare, and receive government
services through the internet. Therefore, restrictions affecting internet access also affect the
exercise of rights guaranteed under Article 19. The Court did not declare access to the internet
itself to be an independent fundamental right. Instead, it held that freedom of speech and the
freedom to practise any profession increasingly depend upon internet access. The Court
introduced several important safeguards.
First, internet shutdowns cannot continue indefinitely.
Second, every shutdown order must be published, enabling affected citizens to challenge it
before the courts.
Third, restrictions must satisfy the constitutional principle of proportionality.
The doctrine of proportionality requires the government to demonstrate that:
● the restriction pursues a legitimate objective;
● there is a rational connection between the restriction and the objective;
● no less restrictive alternative is available; and
● the restriction is proportionate to the public interest sought to be achieved.
This doctrine has become one of the most important tools for judicial review in modern
constitutional law. The Anuradha Bhasin judgment therefore illustrates how constitutional
principles evolve to address technological change. Although the framers of the Constitution
could not have imagined the internet in 1950, the judiciary recognised that constitutional
freedoms must remain meaningful in the digital age. The judgment also demonstrates the delicate
balance courts must maintain between individual liberty and national security. Neither value is
absolute. The Constitution requires both to coexist through reasonable, lawful, and proportionate
restrictions.
Let us now step back and reflect on the journey we have undertaken today.
Our discussion began in 1980 with Bachan Singh, where the Supreme Court confronted one of
the most profound constitutional questions: When, if ever, may the State take a person's life?
The Court responded by establishing the "rarest of rare" doctrine, ensuring that capital
punishment remains an exceptional measure. We then moved to D.K. Basu, where the judiciary
affirmed that constitutional protections do not end at the police station. Even individuals accused
of crimes possess dignity and fundamental rights. The Constitution protects everyone, not only
the innocent. In Vishaka, the Supreme Court demonstrated that constitutional rights cannot
remain ineffective simply because Parliament has not enacted legislation. By framing the
Vishaka Guidelines, the Court ensured that women could work in environments free from sexual
harassment.
The digital era introduced new constitutional challenges. Through Shreya Singhal, the Court
protected freedom of speech against vague and arbitrary restrictions on online expression. In
Anuradha Bhasin, it recognised that constitutional freedoms increasingly depend upon digital
connectivity and that executive restrictions must satisfy the test of proportionality. The judiciary
also expanded the meaning of equality and dignity. Jeeja Ghosh reminded us that persons with
disabilities are entitled not merely to sympathy but to equal participation in society. Shayara
Bano reaffirmed that personal laws must be consistent with constitutional values of equality and
justice. Joseph Shine recognised women as equal partners in marriage and rejected outdated
legal assumptions rooted in patriarchy. Finally, the Nirbhaya case illustrated how judicial
decisions can inspire broader legal reforms. It showed that constitutional values, public
participation, and legislative action can together strengthen the criminal justice system. When
viewed collectively, these judgments reveal an important pattern. The Indian judiciary has
progressively expanded constitutional protections to address new realities. Initially, the focus
was on safeguarding life and personal liberty.
Gradually, attention shifted towards gender equality, human dignity, fair criminal
procedures, disability rights, freedom of expression, and eventually digital constitutionalism.
This evolution demonstrates that the Constitution is not frozen in time.
It continues to grow alongside Indian society. The judiciary has played a central role in ensuring
that constitutional values remain relevant despite changing technologies, social norms, and
political circumstances. However, this also raises an important question.
Should judges actively shape public policy when legislatures fail to act, or should they
exercise greater restraint and leave such decisions to elected representatives?
There is no universally accepted answer. Some scholars praise judicial activism as essential for
protecting fundamental rights, while others caution that courts must respect the separation of
powers. Regardless of one's perspective, one fact remains undeniable. The judgments we have
discussed today have transformed Indian society. They have influenced legislation, changed
administrative practices, expanded individual freedoms, strengthened accountability, and
reaffirmed the Constitution's commitment to justice, liberty, equality, and dignity.
As students of the Indian Constitution, it is important to remember that constitutional law is not
merely about Articles and legal doctrines. It is about people. Every judgment represents the story
of an individual or a community seeking justice. Through these cases, we see how the
Constitution moves beyond the pages of a legal document and becomes a living instrument that
shapes the everyday lives of millions of citizens.

PM WEEK 8 PUBLIC INTEREST LITIGATION
Public Interest Litigation (PIL) in India is a legal mechanism allowing any citizen to file a case
in a High Court (Article 226) or the Supreme Court (Article 32) to protect public interest, social
justice, or the rights of disadvantaged groups. Introduced in the late 1970s, it relaxed the
traditional rule of locus standi, enabling courts to act on behalf of the marginalized.
Key Aspects of PIL in India: Purpose: To enforce public interest, enforce fundamental rights, and
ensure government accountability regarding issues like environmental pollution, human rights,
and social welfare.
Filed Against: State/Central governments, municipal authorities, and sometimes private parties if
they violate public interests.
How to File: A PIL can be filed by any public-spirited individual or organization, not necessarily
the aggrieved party, by submitting a petition in court with supporting evidence.
Significance: It has led to landmark judgments on issues such as environmental protection,
prisoner rights, and gender justice.
Key Constitutional Provisions - In the Supreme Court, PIL is filed under Article 32 for the
enforcement of Fundamental Rights. In the High Courts: Filed under Article 226 for the
enforcement of both Fundamental and other legal rights. InMagistrate Courts: In specific cases
of public nuisance, petitions can be filed under Section 133 of the Criminal Procedure Code.
Historical Development - The origin of PIL is that it was inspired by American jurisprudence, it
took root in India during the late 1970s and 1980s. Pioneers: Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice
V.R. Krishna Iyer were instrumental in its evolution. First Landmark Case: Hussainara Khatoon
v. State of Bihar (1979) led to the release of nearly 40,000 undertrial prisoners and established
the right to a speedy trial. The "Mother of PILs": Kapila Hingorani earned this title for her role in
the Hussainara Khatoon case.
Significance and Features - Epistolary Jurisdiction: The court can treat a simple letter or
postcard addressed to the Chief Justice as a formal writ petition. Non-Adversarial: It is a
collaborative effort between the petitioner, the state, and the court to secure social justice. Suo
Motu Cognizance: The court can initiate a PIL on its own based on news reports or independent
observations. Commissioners: Courts often appoint experts or commissions to investigate facts in
complex public interest cases.
Landmark PIL Cases
Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): Led to guidelines preventing sexual harassment at
workplaces. Key Legal Principles in this PIL was that it upheld Constitutional Rights: The Court
held that sexual harassment is a clear violation of fundamental rights, specifically Article 14 &
15: Right to Equality and Non-discrimination, Article 19(1)(g): Right to practice any profession
or carry out any occupation, and Article 21: Right to Life and Personal Liberty, which includes
the right to live with dignity. Judicial Activism: In the absence of domestic legislation, the Court
filled the "legislative vacuum" by formulating binding guidelines. The Court relied on
international conventions, particularly the CEDAW (Convention on the Elimination of All Forms
of Discrimination Against Women), to interpret Indian constitutional guarantees.
M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987): widely known as the Oleum Gas Leak Case, is a
foundation of Indian environmental law that introduced the revolutionary principle of Absolute
Liability. The Court departed from the 19th-century English rule of "Strict Liability" (from
Rylands v. Fletcher), which allowed for various defences like "Act of God". Under Absolute
Liability, an enterprise engaged in a hazardous activity is strictly and absolutely liable for any
harm caused, with no exceptions allowed. The Court ruled that compensation must be correlated
to the magnitude and financial capacity of the enterprise. A larger, more prosperous company
must pay higher compensation to ensure a "deterrent effect". The judgment expanded the Right
to Life to include the right to live in a clean and healthy environment. The Court reaffirmed its
power to award compensation under Article 32 as a remedial measure for the gross violation of
fundamental rights.
Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984): Focused on the identification and release of
bonded laborers.The Court ruled that Article 21 (Right to Life) includes the right to live with
human dignity, free from exploitation. Article 23 Violations: Forced labour, even if paid, was
held to violate the constitutional prohibition against begar and other forms of forced labour.
Burden of Proof: The Court shifted the burden of proof, stating that if a labourer is found to be
providing forced labour, the court will presume they are a bonded labourer unless the employer
proves otherwise. Relaxation of Locus Standi: The Court reaffirmed that any public-spirited
individual or group can move the Court on behalf of those who, due to poverty or disability,
cannot approach the Court themselves.
Parmanand Katara v. Union of India (1989) is the landmark judgment that established that the
preservation of human life is of paramount importance, superseding all procedural formalities in
medical emergencies. The Court ruled that the right to life is sacrosanct. Every doctor, whether at
a Government hospital or otherwise, has a professional obligation to extend his services with due
expertise for protecting life. No Procedural Barriers: The Court held that a doctor cannot delay
treatment by waiting for police arrival or the completion of legal formalities. The "procedural
law" should not be allowed to operate as a "death trap" for the patient. Professional Ethics: The
judgment highlighted the Code of Medical Ethics (then under the Medical Council of India),
stating that a physician's duty to provide emergency care is absolute. This case is the reason why
hospitals today cannot refuse First Aid or stabilization to accident victims.

JUDICIAL REVIEW
Imagine a situation where a government passes a law that limits free speech, or a university
introduces a rule that unfairly discriminates against certain students, or a state bans something
without clear justification. Who decides whether that action is fair, legal, or constitutional? This
is where judicial review comes in. Judicial review is one of the most powerful tools in a
democracy. It allows courts to examine laws and government actions and decide whether they
are consistent with the Constitution.
Hello learners, welcome back, I am Amrita and today we shall be diving deep into a feature
namely - Judicial Review.
In simple terms, judicial review means the power of the judiciary to review the actions of the
legislature and the executive and declare them invalid if they violate the Constitution. It is not
about judges running the country. It is about ensuring that every organ of government stays
within constitutional limits. In India, this power flows from Articles 13, 32, and 226 of the
Constitution of India. Article 13 clearly states that any law inconsistent with fundamental rights
shall be void. Articles 32 and 226 empower the Supreme Court and High Courts to enforce those
rights.
The idea of judicial review is not uniquely Indian. It has roots in the famous American case of
Marbury v. Madison in 1803, where the US Supreme Court asserted its authority to strike down
unconstitutional laws. But in India, judicial review is not just a judicial invention. It is written
into the Constitution itself. That means it is not optional; it is a foundational principle of the
constitutional system.
To understand why judicial review matters, we need to understand constitutional supremacy. In
India, the Constitution is the highest law of the land. Parliament is powerful, but it is not
supreme. It cannot make laws that violate fundamental rights or alter the basic structure of the
Constitution. Judicial review ensures that this supremacy of the Constitution is maintained.
One of the earliest and most important cases on judicial review in India was Kesavananda
Bharati v. State of Kerala. In this 1973 judgment, the Supreme Court introduced the Basic
Structure Doctrine. The Court held that while Parliament has wide powers to amend the
Constitution under Article 368, it cannot alter its basic structure. This basic structure includes
principles like democracy, secularism, rule of law, and judicial review itself. In other words,
judicial review was declared so essential that even Parliament cannot remove it.
Another landmark case that shows judicial review in action is Minerva Mills v. Union of India.
In this case, the Supreme Court struck down certain amendments made during the Emergency
that tried to limit judicial review. The Court emphasized that limited government and judicial
review are part of the basic structure. This case reinforced the idea that courts are not enemies of
Parliament but guardians of constitutional balance.
Judicial review does not apply only to constitutional amendments. It also applies to ordinary
laws and executive actions. For example, in Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, the Supreme
Court expanded the interpretation of Article 21, which protects the right to life and personal
liberty. The Court held that any procedure depriving a person of liberty must be “just, fair, and
reasonable.” This decision transformed Article 21 and allowed courts to review state actions
more deeply. It showed how judicial review can evolve rights over time.
High Courts also exercise strong powers of judicial review. Under Article 226, they can review
state laws, administrative orders, and even actions of public authorities. In Naz Foundation v.
Government of NCT of Delhi, the Delhi High Court used judicial review to read down Section
377 of the Indian Penal Code, stating that criminalising consensual same-sex relationships
violated fundamental rights. Although the journey of that issue continued to the Supreme Court
in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, the Delhi High Court’s intervention demonstrated how
judicial review can challenge deeply rooted social norms.
Judicial review is also crucial in checking arbitrary executive actions. In the case of Justice K.S.
Puttaswamy v. Union of India, the Supreme Court declared that the right to privacy is a
fundamental right under the Constitution. This judgment had major implications for data
protection, surveillance, and personal autonomy. It arose from concerns about the Aadhaar
scheme and showed how judicial review can respond to technological and digital challenges in
modern society.
Another powerful example is the case of Shreya Singhal v. Union of India. The Supreme Court
struck down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act because it violated the freedom of
speech under Article 19(1)(a). The provision was vague and allowed arrests for online speech
that was merely “offensive.” By using judicial review, the Court protected digital free expression
for millions of users.
Judicial review is not just about striking down laws. Sometimes, courts interpret laws in a way
that makes them constitutional. This is called “reading down.” Instead of completely invalidating
a law, the court narrows its scope so that it aligns with constitutional principles. This approach
reflects judicial restraint and respect for legislative intent.
However, judicial review is not without controversy. Critics argue that it can lead to judicial
overreach, where courts step into policy domains that should be left to elected representatives.
Supporters respond that when fundamental rights are at stake, courts must act, even if the issue is
politically sensitive. The balance between activism and restraint is an ongoing debate in
constitutional law.
For young citizens, judicial review is deeply relevant. It affects issues like internet freedom,
campus regulations, gender equality, environmental protection, and even exam policies. When
courts review government decisions on internet shutdowns, environmental clearances, or
reservation policies, they are exercising judicial review. These decisions shape everyday life in
ways we may not always notice.
Judicial review also strengthens accountability. It sends a message that power is never absolute.
Whether it is Parliament, a state legislature, a government department, or a public official,
everyone must act within constitutional boundaries. This promotes transparency and discourages
arbitrary governance.
At the same time, judicial review depends on an independent judiciary. If judges are influenced
by political pressure, the entire system weakens. That is why constitutional safeguards around
judicial appointments, tenure, and removal are so important. Judicial review works effectively
only when courts are free to decide without fear or favour.
In India’s democratic structure, judicial review acts like a safety valve. It prevents the
concentration of power and protects minority rights against majoritarian impulses. Democracy is
not just about majority rule; it is also about constitutional limits. Judicial review ensures that
these limits are meaningful.
Ultimately, judicial review keeps the Constitution alive. It transforms constitutional text into
lived reality. It ensures that rights are not just promises on paper but enforceable guarantees.
Every time a court strikes down an unjust law, interprets a right expansively, or questions
arbitrary power, it reinforces the idea that the Constitution is supreme.
For a young audience, the takeaway is simple but powerful: judicial review is your constitutional
shield. It is the reason you can question unfair rules, challenge discrimination, and demand
accountability. It is the mechanism that turns citizens into rights-bearing individuals rather than
passive subjects.
Judicial review is not about judges versus the government. It is about the Constitution guiding
everyone. It is about maintaining a balance where power is exercised responsibly and rights are
respected consistently. In that sense, judicial review is not just a legal doctrine. It is one of the
strongest pillars holding up India’s democratic structure.

In today’s session, we will explore one of the most debated and dynamic concepts
within the study of the Indian Constitution — judicial activism. We will examine what
judicial activism means, how it evolved in India, its constitutional foundations, major
examples, criticisms, and its broader implications for democracy and separation of
powers.
Let us begin with a simple question.
Should courts only interpret the law exactly as it is written?
Or should they step in when the legislature and executive fail to act — especially in
matters involving fundamental rights?
The answer to this question lies at the heart of judicial activism.
When we think about constitutional authority in India, we think of the Supreme Court of
India and the High Courts of India. These courts are entrusted with interpreting the
Constitution, protecting fundamental rights, and ensuring that state actions remain
within constitutional limits.
Judicial activism refers to a proactive role played by the judiciary in interpreting and
enforcing constitutional principles, often going beyond a strict textual reading of the law.
It involves judicial innovation, expansive interpretation of rights, and, at times, directions
to the executive or legislature to fill policy gaps.
However, judicial activism must be understood in context.
In the early years after independence, the judiciary adopted a relatively conservative
approach. The courts were cautious and often deferred to parliamentary sovereignty.
For example, in the early land reform cases, the judiciary upheld legislative supremacy
in many instances.
The turning point came in the 1970s.
One of the most significant moments in Indian constitutional history was the case of
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala in 1973. In this case, the Supreme Court
propounded the Basic Structure Doctrine, holding that while Parliament has wide
powers to amend the Constitution under Article 368, it cannot alter its basic structure.
This was a classic example of judicial activism.
The Court effectively placed substantive limits on Parliament’s amending power —
something not explicitly stated in the constitutional text. The doctrine protected core
features such as democracy, secularism, rule of law, and judicial review.
The period of the Emergency between 1975 and 1977 further shaped the trajectory of
judicial activism. During the Emergency, in the infamous ADM Jabalpur case, the
Supreme Court held that the right to life could be suspended. This decision was widely
criticized and later regarded as a failure of judicial responsibility.
After the Emergency, the judiciary adopted a more assertive stance in order to restore
public confidence.
This phase saw the emergence of Public Interest Litigation, or PIL.
Through PIL, the Supreme Court and High Courts relaxed the traditional rule of locus
standi. Earlier, only an aggrieved party could approach the court. With PIL, any
public-spirited individual could file a petition on behalf of marginalized communities.
For example, cases concerning bonded labor, prison conditions, environmental
degradation, and child labor were taken up by the courts even when the affected
individuals could not directly approach them.
The expansion of Article 21 of the Constitution — the right to life and personal liberty —
is one of the strongest illustrations of judicial activism. Initially interpreted narrowly,
Article 21 was later expanded to include the Right To Livelihood, The Right To Clean
Environment, The Right To Privacy, And The Right To Dignity.
In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, the Supreme Court held that “procedure
established by law” must be just, fair, and reasonable. This interpretation transformed
Article 21 into a powerful tool for protecting civil liberties.
Similarly, in Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan, the Court laid down guidelines against sexual
harassment at the workplace in the absence of parliamentary legislation. These
guidelines remained in force until Parliament enacted a law on the subject.
In this case, the judiciary effectively filled a legislative vacuum.
Judicial activism has also played a significant role in environmental governance. The
Court has issued directions concerning pollution control, forest conservation, and river
cleaning projects. Through continuing mandamus, the judiciary has monitored
compliance over extended periods.
However, judicial activism is not without criticism.
Critics argue that excessive activism leads to judicial overreach — a situation where
courts encroach upon the domain of the legislature or executive. In a democratic system
based on separation of powers, policymaking is primarily the responsibility of elected
representatives. When courts begin issuing detailed administrative directives, questions
arise about democratic legitimacy and institutional competence.
For instance, when courts intervene in matters of economic policy, appointment
processes, or administrative restructuring, some argue that they risk substituting judicial
wisdom for political accountability.
Another criticism relates to selective activism. Courts may take up certain issues
enthusiastically while neglecting systemic judicial reforms such as reducing case
backlog or addressing infrastructure deficiencies.
Thus, the debate is not about whether judicial activism is good or bad. The real question
is: where should the line be drawn?
Judicial activism becomes necessary when constitutional values are threatened and
other branches fail to act. However, activism must be guided by constitutional morality,
institutional restraint, and respect for democratic processes.
From a theoretical perspective, judicial activism reflects a dynamic understanding of the
Constitution as a living document. The Indian Constitution is not merely a legal text but
a transformative charter aimed at achieving Social Justice, Equality, And Dignity.
The judiciary, therefore, has sometimes assumed the role of a guardian of
transformative constitutionalism.
At the same time, judicial restraint remains an equally important principle. Courts must
recognize their limitations, especially in areas requiring technical expertise or
large-scale policy planning.
In recent years, the conversation around judicial activism has evolved. The recognition
of the right to privacy as a fundamental right in 2017 reaffirmed the judiciary’s proactive
stance in safeguarding individual autonomy in the digital age. At the same time, debates
continue regarding judicial appointments, accountability, and the balance of power
between institutions.
In conclusion, judicial activism in India has shaped the trajectory of Constitutional
Governance. It has expanded Fundamental Rights, Strengthened Accountability, and
Protected Vulnerable Communities. At critical moments in history, it has served as a
counter-majoritarian safeguard against executive or legislative excess.
Yet, judicial activism must operate within the framework of constitutional boundaries and
democratic principles.
The strength of the Indian Constitution lies not only in its text but in how institutions
interpret and apply it. Judicial activism represents one dimension of that interpretative
journey — a journey marked by innovation, controversy, and constitutional evolution.

The story of the Indian Constitution begins long before 1950. It emerges from India’s long
struggle against colonial rule and its aspiration for self-governance. Nearly two centuries of
British rule shaped India’s administrative and legal systems, especially through laws like the
Government of India Act 1935, which laid down a federal structure, provincial autonomy,
emergency provisions, and the office of the Governor. Many of these structural features were
later refined and incorporated into independent India’s Constitution.
As independence approached, the Constituent Assembly of India was formed in 1946 to draft a
Constitution for a free nation. Chaired by Rajendra Prasad and with the Drafting Committee led
by B. R. Ambedkar, the Assembly, worked for nearly three years. They debated every clause
intensely, ensuring that the final document reflected justice, equality, and unity in a deeply
diverse society. Despite the trauma of Partition, the framers succeeded in creating a document
that would bind the nation together.
The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into effect on 26 January 1950,
marking India’s transformation into a sovereign republic. At its heart lies the Preamble — the
philosophical soul of the Constitution. Beginning with the powerful words “We, the People of
India,” it declares India to be a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic. These words
are not decorative; they define the nation’s identity.
“Sovereign” affirms India’s complete independence in internal and external affairs. “Socialist,”
added later through the 42nd Amendment, reflects a commitment to social and economic justice.
“Secular” guarantees equal respect for all religions. “Democratic” ensures that power flows from
the people through elections. And “Republic” means that the head of state is elected, not
hereditary.
The Preamble also promises Justice — social, economic, and political; Liberty of thought and
expression; Equality of status and opportunity; and Fraternity assuring dignity and unity. These
ideals guide not only laws but also the interpretation of the Constitution. In the landmark
Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala case, the Supreme Court established the Basic Structure
Doctrine, holding that Parliament cannot amend the Constitution in a way that destroys its core
principles. This ensured that the spirit of the Preamble would remain protected.
One of the most remarkable aspects of the Indian Constitution is its length and detail. It is the
longest written Constitution in the world, carefully crafted to govern a country of immense
diversity — linguistic, religious, regional, and cultural. Its detailed provisions allow it to adapt
and respond to changing needs through amendments, balancing rigidity with flexibility.
India follows a parliamentary system inspired by the British model, where the executive is
accountable to the legislature. At the same time, it has a federal structure with a unitary bias.
Powers are divided between the Centre and the States through Union, State, and Concurrent
Lists, yet during emergencies, the Centre can assume greater control to maintain stability.
The Constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights such as equality before law, freedom of speech,
protection against exploitation, and the right to constitutional remedies. These rights are
enforceable in courts. Alongside them, Directive Principles of State Policy guide the government
in promoting social welfare, economic justice, and equitable development. Though not
enforceable in court, they shape major policies like rural employment schemes and education
reforms.
Another unique feature is Fundamental Duties, reminding citizens that democracy is sustained
not only by claiming rights but also by fulfilling responsibilities. Universal adult franchise
ensures that every citizen above 18 has the right to vote, making India the world’s largest
democracy from day one. Single citizenship promotes national unity, while special provisions for
certain states protect cultural and regional diversity.
An independent judiciary acts as the guardian of the Constitution, empowered with judicial
review to strike down unconstitutional laws. The impeachment process for the President and
removal procedures for judges reflect accountability at the highest levels.
The Indian Constitution is also a global document in inspiration. It borrows the parliamentary
system and rule of law from the United Kingdom, fundamental rights and judicial review from
the United States Constitution, Directive Principles from the Constitution of Ireland, federalism
with a strong centre from the Constitution of Canada, and ideals of liberty, equality, and
fraternity from the French Revolution. Yet, despite these borrowings, it remains uniquely Indian
in spirit and design.
Ultimately, the Indian Constitution is more than a legal document. It is a living framework for
governance, a moral vision for society, and a promise between the state and its citizens. It has
guided India through wars, political crises, social change, and economic transformation,
remaining resilient and relevant.
As citizens, we are not just beneficiaries of this Constitution — we are its guardians. Every vote
we cast, every right we exercise responsibly, and every act of fraternity we practice keeps its
spirit alive.
